# CG L02: Give ChatGPT a Goal, Context, Output, and Boundaries

## What you will finish

Write prompts with a goal, context, output format, and boundaries so useful results arrive with less back-and-forth. When you are done you will have one vague prompt from your own history rewritten into four labeled parts, with the old result next to the new result so you can see exactly what changed.

## Why this matters

"Write me an email about fall aeration." That is a real prompt a lawn care owner in Gilmer showed me. He typed it at nine at night after a full day on the mower. What came back opened with "Transform your lawn this autumn!" and offered a discount he never mentioned. He closed the laptop and went to bed.

The tool did not fail. The prompt did. ChatGPT filled every gap he left with the most average thing on the internet. When you do not say who the reader is, it guesses. When you do not say what not to claim, it claims.

The fix is not a magic phrase. It is four parts, in order, every time. Goal, context, output, boundaries. Ten minutes writing the prompt saves forty minutes of "no, not like that." After a few weeks it takes two minutes because the frame is in your head.

This lesson comes second because the Project from lesson one now holds your durable facts. The prompt only has to carry what is specific to this one task. That is why it stays short.

## The lesson

### 1. State the result

The goal is what must be finished. Not what you want help with. Finished means you could hand it to a person and they could use it.

"Help me with an email" is not a goal. "Goal: a 120-word email to past customers, ready to paste into my email tool, that gets them to book fall aeration by replying or calling" is a goal. It names the piece, the reader, and the action the reader takes.

Put the reader in the goal. Put the action in the goal. If you cannot say what the reader should do after reading, you are not ready to write yet, and that is worth knowing before you spend the time.

What good looks like: one sentence, and a stranger could tell you when it is done.

The mistake is writing activities instead of results. "Brainstorm ideas." "Give me some options." Those keep you in the chat for an hour. If you really need options, make that the result: "Goal: three subject lines, each under 45 characters, for the email below."

Example: a dentist in Kilgore wants to remind lapsed patients to book a cleaning. Goal: "A friendly 80-word text message, sent from the office number, that gets a patient who has not visited in 18 months to call and book a cleaning."

### 2. Supply only relevant context

Context is the facts, the audience, and the source material for this specific task. Your project instructions already carry who you are and how you talk. Do not paste that again. Add what is new: the offer, the date, the price if you are publishing one, the season, the reader's situation, and any real material you have.

Paste real material instead of describing it. If a customer asked a question, paste the question. If you have notes from the job, paste the notes. ChatGPT works better from your actual words than from your summary of your words.

The rule for what to include: if the fact would change the output, include it. If it would not, leave it out. Then label where each fact came from, the same way you did in your project instructions. "Price: $85 per yard up to a quarter acre (source: my 2026 price sheet)."

What good looks like: five to ten lines of facts, each one true today, none of them decoration.

The mistake runs both directions. Some people paste their whole website and a three-year-old brochure, so the draft carries old prices. Others paste nothing and then get mad when it guesses. Relevant means the middle.

Example: for the aeration email, context is the service, the price, the booking method, the towns served, when the season runs, and the fact that the reader used the company before. That is it.

### 3. Specify format and limits

Output is the exact shape of the thing. Format, length, sections. "Output: one subject line plus a 120-word body. Plain text. One link placeholder written as [BOOKING LINK]. No bullet points. No sign-off longer than my name." Say it the way you would say it to a new hire who has never seen your emails.

Boundaries are what it must not do and what it must ask. Four kinds, and the prompt below has all four. Facts not to invent: no prices, reviews, statistics, or awards that are not in the context. Claims to avoid: no "best in East Texas," no results promises, nothing about the weather. Tools or sources required: "use only the fact sheet in this project." Questions to ask: the last line of the prompt makes ChatGPT list any missing fact that would materially change the result before it drafts.

That last line is the cheapest quality control you will ever run. Read the list, answer what matters, say "go." Skip it and the gaps get filled with guesses.

What good looks like: you can hand the output to someone without editing the shape, and nothing in it makes a claim you would not say to a customer's face.

The mistake is skipping boundaries because they feel negative. Then the draft says "over 500 five-star reviews" and you never had that many. Boundaries are not pessimism. They are the part of the prompt that protects your name.

Example: "Boundaries: do not invent a discount. Do not mention frost dates. Use only the facts above. Before drafting, list any missing fact."

Put the four parts together and you have the whole prompt. Keep the labels. They make the prompt readable by you in six months and by whoever you hand the business to.

## Worked example

Gilmer Lawn and Land is a fictional example. Marcus runs it with one crew. His vague prompt was "write an email about fall aeration."

Here is the rewrite he used inside his project chat. Goal: a 120-word email to past customers that gets them to book fall aeration by replying or calling. Context: core aeration, $85 per yard up to a quarter acre (source: 2026 price sheet), Gilmer and Gladewater, booking by phone or reply, season runs September through October, reader used us in the last two years. Output: one subject line under 45 characters plus a 120-word plain text body, one call to action, no bullets. Boundaries: do not invent discounts, reviews, or results; do not mention weather or frost dates; use only these facts; before drafting, list any missing fact that would materially change the result.

ChatGPT came back with three missing facts: the phone number, whether overseeding is included, and whether there is a booking deadline. He answered: office number, overseeding is a separate quote, no deadline. Then it drafted.

Example output:

```text
Subject: Fall aeration for your yard
Hi [First name],
You had us out in the last couple of years, so you know the drill. Fall is when core aeration does the most good for a Gilmer lawn.
It is $85 per yard up to a quarter acre. Overseeding is a separate quote if you want it.
Reply to this email or call 903-555-0127 and we will get you on the schedule for September or October.
Marcus
```

He checked it against the pass standard. Could a new reader find the goal, the trusted context, the expected output, and the boundaries without guessing? Yes, because the labels are right there in the prompt.

The one revision: the first draft opened with "before the first freeze hits in late October." He had said no frost dates, but the draft found another way to predict weather. He added "no seasonal predictions of any kind" to the boundaries and reran it. Clean.

He put the old result and the new result side by side. The old one was longer, invented a 15 percent discount, and never gave a phone number.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Goal: [what must be finished]. Context: [facts, audience, source material]. Output: [exact format, length, and sections]. Boundaries: [facts not to invent, claims to avoid, tools or sources required, and questions to ask]. Before drafting, list any missing fact that would materially change the result.
```

## Guided practice

1. Find one prompt from your own ChatGPT history that gave you something you could not use.
2. Rewrite it with the four labels using the four-part prompt card from the workbook.
3. Run it in a chat inside your project, answer the missing facts it lists, then say go.
4. Paste the old result and the new result into one document, side by side.
5. Write one line under them: what changed, and which part of the prompt caused it.

## Assignment and evidence

Rewrite one vague prompt with all four parts and compare the first result to the revised result.

Save the source material, the prompt, the first result, the final result, and your review notes.

## Pass standard

A new reader can identify the goal, trusted context, expected output, and non-negotiable boundaries without guessing.

## Common mistakes

- Writing an activity as the goal. Fix: name the finished piece, the reader, and the action the reader takes.
- Pasting the whole website as context. Fix: include only facts that would change this output, and label each source.
- Leaving out the format. Fix: state length, sections, and what a placeholder looks like.
- Skipping boundaries because they feel negative. Fix: list what not to invent and what not to claim, every time.
- Ignoring the missing-facts list. Fix: answer it before you let it draft.
