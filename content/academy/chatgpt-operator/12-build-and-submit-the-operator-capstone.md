# CG L12: Build and Submit the Operator Capstone

## What you will finish

Build a complete ChatGPT-assisted business system that moves from approved input to a reviewed asset, owned record, and measurable next action. When you are done you will have one viewable link that holds the whole system: the brief, the sources, the prompts, a finished deliverable, the quality evidence, the safety review, the runbook, and the number you will watch.

## Why this matters

Eleven lessons ago you had a chat window and a habit of retyping your business every time. Now you have a project that remembers, a prompt frame, a content kit, an image, a landing page, a research log, a client document, an audit loop, a written workflow, and a safety review. Those are parts. The capstone is the machine.

Here is why it has to be one link. A system that lives in your head is not a system. It is you. The plumber in Marshall who set up the first project in lesson one now has a workflow his office manager runs on Thursdays without calling him, because it is written down in one place she can open.

The capstone is also how you get reviewed. A reviewer at The LeadFlow Pro opens your link, reads the runbook, checks the deliverable against the sources, and either approves it or tells you exactly what is missing. That approval is the completion credential for this course.

You have built most of it already. This lesson assembles it, fills the gaps, and ships it.

## The lesson

### 1. Choose one real workflow

Take the workflow from lesson ten, the one you ran twice and reviewed for safety in lesson eleven. That is your capstone. Do not start a new one. Pick the one that runs every week and saves real time, because the reviewer will ask what result it produced, and "it was interesting" is not a result.

The workflow must move through the whole chain. Approved input: a brief, form, or notes you signed off on. Reviewed asset: what ChatGPT drafted and a human checked. Owned record: saved where you control it, with a name and a date. Measurable next action: one thing that happens because the asset exists, and one number you can count.

If your lesson ten workflow does not reach a next action, extend it. The estimate reply ends with a site visit on the calendar. The welcome email ends with a booked appointment.

Open a new chat in your project named "Capstone." Run the exact working prompt below. It puts ChatGPT in the reviewer's chair and has it interview you one question at a time until it can produce the twelve pieces listed in the prompt. Answer each question from what you already built. Where you do not know, say so. The last line forbids inventing business facts, and that applies to you too.

What good looks like: you can say in one sentence what goes in, what comes out, who checks it, where it lives, and what number moves.

The mistake is choosing a workflow you have never run. The interview exposes that in about four questions.

Example: a realtor in Henderson picks her weekly listing update email, not the ad campaign she keeps meaning to start.

### 2. Create the full evidence package

The package is twelve items in one folder. You have most of them already.

Approved brief: the source brief from lesson three, or the reader and decision line from lesson eight. Source register: the verification log from lesson seven or the file inventory from lesson eight, every input by name and date. Project instructions: copied out of the project, with the safety line from lesson eleven in place. Working prompt: the four-part prompt from lesson two, exactly as the workflow uses it. Output schema: the sections or fields the output always has.

Safety rules: the safe, caution, and prohibited list from lesson eleven. Quality checklist: the five-line review from lesson ten, or the audit scorecard from lesson nine. Example deliverable: one real output from a real run, redacted if it has customer details. Storage plan: the folder, the file naming, the log. Next action: what happens after approval. Metric: one number, counted how often, by whom. Failure recovery: what the workflow does when input is missing or the output fails the checklist.

Put every item in a folder you own. Google Drive or a Notion page works. Name each file for what it is: 01-brief, 02-source-register, and so on. Then write the runbook: one page, in order, from trigger to metric. Someone who has never seen ChatGPT should be able to follow it with the project open. The reviewer reads it first.

Before you share, run the audit loop from lesson nine on the runbook itself. Requirements are the twelve items. Evidence is the folder. Fix what is missing.

What good looks like: twelve files and a runbook, and none of them are placeholders.

The mistake is writing the package from memory instead of from the artifacts. The reviewer checks the example deliverable against the source register. If they do not match, the capstone fails.

Example: the fence company's folder holds a redacted estimate reply, the log spreadsheet, and "site visits scheduled per week" as the metric.

### 3. Submit the artifact and runbook for review

Set the folder or page to viewable by anyone with the link. Open it in a private browser window to make sure it works without a login. Check that it holds no secrets or unredacted customer information.

Paste the one link into the submission panel below. One link. Not five.

Keep running the workflow while you wait. The reviewer looks for the five things in the pass standard: a useful result, repeatability, source truth, human control, and something they can independently review. If a piece is thin, they will say which one, and you fix it and resubmit.

Here is what you have when it passes. A documented system your business owns. It runs on your project, your files, your prompts, and your review. If you changed tools tomorrow the runbook would still describe the work. That is the point of this course. You are not renting a process. You wrote it.

Next: run it for four weeks and watch the metric. Then pick the next weekly task and build the same package. The second one takes a third of the time. If you are not sure which task is next, the Business Growth Diagnostic at /diagnostic will point at the leak.

The next course is OA02 Content Engine. It takes the content kit from lesson three and the voice work from lesson five and turns them into a weekly publishing system. Bring your project, your voice sample, and your capstone runbook.

The mistake is submitting drafts. Every item should be the version that actually ran.

## Worked example

Sabine Valley Plumbing in Marshall is the fictional example from lesson one. Dale's capstone is the water heater estimate follow-up workflow he wrote in lesson ten and reviewed in lesson eleven. Trigger: a water heater estimate given on site. Input: the tech's notes. Output: a follow-up email with the quote and one next action. Reviewer: office manager Lisa. Storage: a Drive folder and a log sheet. Next action: a follow-up call date on the calendar.

He opens a chat named "Capstone" and runs the exact working prompt with the workflow and business filled in. ChatGPT asks one question at a time: what starts it, what the notes contain, who reviews, where it is saved, what he counts.

Example output:

```text
Question 7: What single number will tell you this workflow is working, and how often will you count it?
Dale: Estimates followed up within 24 hours, counted every Friday by Lisa.
Question 8: What happens when the tech's notes are missing the model or the price?
Dale: The draft is not created. Lisa texts the tech for the missing item, and the log shows "waiting on notes."
I now have enough for the twelve items. Here is the approved brief, followed by the source register.
```

Dale builds the folder with all twelve items. The source register names the tech notes template and the current price sheet by date. The safety rules strip the customer address from drafts. The example deliverable is one redacted follow-up email from a real run. He writes the one-page runbook and runs the lesson nine audit on it. Two gaps: no failure step for a stale price sheet, and no metric owner. He adds both.

Review against the pass standard. Useful business result: follow-ups now go out within a day instead of whenever. Repeatable: Lisa runs it. Source truth preserved: every quote number traces to the tech notes. Human control: Lisa sends every email. Independently reviewable: one link, runbook on top.

The one revision that made it better: his first metric was "more water heater jobs." He changed it to "estimates followed up within 24 hours" because he can count that on Friday and the workflow controls it. He shares the folder, tests the link in a private window, and submits.

## Pass standard

The capstone produces a useful business result, is repeatable, preserves source truth, includes human control, and can be independently reviewed.

## Common mistakes

- Starting a new, more impressive workflow for the capstone. Fix: submit the one you have already run twice.
- A metric you cannot count. Fix: one number, counted on a set day, by a named person.
- A package written from memory. Fix: every item is the version that actually ran, copied from the artifact.
- Customer details left in the example deliverable. Fix: redact before you share the link.
- Five links instead of one. Fix: one folder or page, runbook on top, everything else inside.
