# CG L05: Write Social Posts and Emails That Sound Human

## What you will finish

Write channel-specific posts and emails that preserve the operator's real voice, story, and next action. When you are done you will have one Facebook post and one email built from the same real source note, both read aloud, with three specific changes marked that made them sound like you.

## Why this matters

You can spot an AI post from across the room. "Exciting news!" "We are thrilled to announce." "Elevate your experience." Your customers can spot it too, and it costs you something you cannot buy back: the sense that a real person is on the other end.

A detailer in Kilgore had this problem. Her texts to customers were great. Short, funny, specific. "Found a wedding ring under your seat. It is in the cup holder. You are welcome." Then she started using ChatGPT for Facebook and every post sounded like a car dealership. Engagement dropped and she figured people did not care about detailing posts. They cared. They just could not hear her anymore.

The fix is not to stop using the tool. The fix is to feed it your real language and a real thing that happened, make it write for one person, then read the result out loud and cut anything you would never say.

This comes after the content kit because the kit taught structure. This lesson teaches voice, and voice is what makes the structure worth reading.

## The lesson

### 1. Capture real source language

ChatGPT can only match a voice it has seen. So show it yours.

Collect a writing sample. Three to five texts you sent customers this month. A Facebook post you wrote yourself, before any tool. An email you typed in a hurry. Hurried writing is the most honest sample you have, because you were not performing.

Then get a source note. A source note is one real thing that happened this week, in your words. The job that ran long. The question a customer asked twice. The truck that showed up with two years of dog hair. If typing is slow, record a voice memo on your phone while you explain it to an imaginary customer, use your phone's transcription, and paste the text. Do not clean it up. The mess is the voice.

Put the writing sample and the source note together in a new chat inside your project, named for the piece. If you will use this sample often, save it as a file in the project called voice-sample-2026.txt so every future chat can use it.

What good looks like: a stranger reading the sample could describe how you talk in three sentences.

The mistake is giving ChatGPT a description of your voice instead of a sample. "Friendly but professional" describes ten million businesses. Twelve real sentences describe one.

Example: a lawn crew owner in Gladewater pastes four texts and a voice memo transcript about a sprinkler head he found with the mower. That is enough.

### 2. Write for one reader

The exact working prompt below does two things in order. First it studies your sample and describes the voice using observable traits. Not "warm." Things it can point to. "Sentences average eight words. Starts with what happened, not with a greeting. Uses 'y'all' once per message. Never uses exclamation points. Ends with a plain instruction."

Read that description before you read anything else. If it is wrong, correct it in the chat. "I do use exclamation points, about one per post." That correction improves everything after it.

Then it writes one Facebook post and one email for the audience and goal you name. Name one reader, not a crowd. Not "homeowners." "Pam in Longview, whose water heater died Tuesday, who has never called a plumber before and is worried about the price." One reader makes the writing specific. Specific reads as human.

Give it the goal the way you did in lesson two: the piece, the reader, the action. The prompt already forbids em dashes, fake quotes, invented experiences, and motivational filler, and it ends every piece with one useful next action.

Facebook and email are different rooms. A post is you talking to people who follow you, so it can start in the middle of the story. An email lands in a crowded inbox, so the subject line has to earn the open and the body has to get to the point in two lines.

What good looks like: the post could only have come from your business, and the email could only be about this week.

The mistake is writing for everyone. The moment the reader is "our valued customers," the writing goes flat. Put a name on it.

Example: the detailer names her reader "a guy in Kilgore who drives a work truck, has a dog, and thinks detailing is for show cars." The post opens with the dog hair.

### 3. Read aloud and remove generic phrasing

Now the part almost nobody does. Read both pieces out loud. Actually out loud, with your mouth. Not in your head.

Your ear catches what your eye skips. Anything you stumble on, anything that makes you feel like you are reading a script, anything you would never say to a customer standing in front of you. Those get cut or rewritten in your own words.

The usual offenders: "we pride ourselves," "look no further," "your satisfaction is our priority," "elevate," "we go above and beyond," "exciting news," "don't miss out." Also the sentence that is true but not yours, like a story detail you did not tell it. If it is not in the source note, it did not happen. Cut it.

Make the fixes yourself, or tell ChatGPT the exact line and the exact replacement. "Replace 'we go above and beyond' with 'we found the ring and put it in the cup holder.'" Then read it aloud once more.

Mark three changes. This is the assignment. Three places where a specific edit made it sound more like you. Write them down with the before and the after. Those three lines are the start of a personal do-not-say list you will use in every lesson after this one.

What good looks like: you could record the post as a video without changing a word.

The mistake is polishing until it is smooth. Smooth is generic. Your real writing has edges. Keep them.

Example: a plumber in Marshall reads his email aloud and hears "our team of experienced professionals." He has two guys. He changes it to "me and Cody." Done.

## Worked example

Redbird Detailing in Kilgore is a fictional example. Tasha runs it out of a two-bay shop. Her writing sample is four texts, including the wedding ring one. Her source note, typed fast: "Guy brought in a 2019 F-150, said it was a little dirty. Two years of lab hair in the seats. Took three hours with the extractor. Found a wedding ring under the passenger seat. Called him before I even finished. He had been looking for it since Christmas."

She opens a chat named "Ring truck post and email," pastes the sample and the note into the exact working prompt, and sets the audience and goal: "Truck owners in Kilgore and Overton who think detailing is only for show cars. Goal: get them to text a photo of their interior for a quote at 903-555-0163."

Example output:

```text
Voice traits: short sentences, mostly under ten words. Starts with the event, not a greeting. Dry humor without exclamation points. Signs off with "you are welcome." Ends with a plain instruction.
Facebook post: A guy dropped off a 2019 F-150 last week and said it was a little dirty. Two years of lab hair. Three hours with the extractor. Also found a wedding ring under the passenger seat that had been missing since Christmas. If your truck is a little dirty, text a photo of the inside to 903-555-0163 and I will send you a quote. Let us restore your ride.
Email subject: Your truck is probably not as clean as you think
```

She reads both aloud. The post is close until the last line. "Let us restore your ride" is not something she has ever said. She replaces it with "You are welcome," which is how she actually signs off. The email body had "we treat every vehicle like it is our own." Cut. It also said "our trained technicians." It is her and a part-timer. Changed to "me."

Those are her three marked changes. Review against the pass standard: source-backed details, yes, every fact came from the note; fits the channel, the post starts mid-story and the email leads with a subject line that earns the open; credible aloud, yes, after the three cuts. She saves the sample, the note, both drafts, both finals, and the three changes with before and after.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Study this writing sample and source note: [paste]. First describe the voice using observable traits. Then write one Facebook post and one email for [audience and goal]. Preserve the natural sentence rhythm and plain language. Do not use em dashes, fake quotes, invented experiences, or generic motivational filler. End with one useful next action.
```

## Guided practice

1. Collect three to five texts or posts you wrote yourself and paste them into a new project chat.
2. Type or transcribe a source note about one real thing that happened this week, and do not clean it up.
3. Run the exact working prompt with one named reader and one goal, and correct the voice description if it is off.
4. Read both pieces out loud and cut every line you would never say.
5. Write down three before-and-after changes and add them to your voice and channel checklist.

## Assignment and evidence

Create a post and email from the same source note, read both aloud, and highlight three changes that made them sound more like you.

Save the source material, the prompt, the first result, the final result, and your review notes.

## Pass standard

The writing uses source-backed details, fits the channel, and sounds credible when spoken aloud.

## Common mistakes

- Describing your voice instead of showing it. Fix: paste real texts and posts you wrote without help.
- Writing for "our customers." Fix: name one reader with one situation.
- Letting a story detail in that you never told it. Fix: if it is not in the source note, cut it.
- Reading silently. Fix: read out loud and trust your ear.
- Sanding off every edge. Fix: keep the phrases that are actually yours, even if they are odd.
