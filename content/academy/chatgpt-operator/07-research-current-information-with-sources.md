# CG L07: Research Current Information With Sources

## What you will finish

Research changing information with current sources, dates, direct links, and a clear separation between evidence and inference. When you are done you will have one business decision researched, every cited source opened and logged, and a short write-up that says what the evidence supports and what it does not.

## Why this matters

ChatGPT will answer any question with confidence. That is the problem. Ask it what a software subscription costs, what a permit requires, or what a competitor charges, and without web search it answers from memory, and the memory might be two years old. Prices change. Rules change. Companies get bought.

A coffee shop owner in Kilgore almost signed up for an online ordering platform based on a fee ChatGPT quoted her. The real fee, on the vendor's own pricing page, was different. Not by a lot, but enough over a year to matter. She caught it because she clicked the link.

With web search on, ChatGPT can find current pages and cite them. But a citation is not proof. It is a pointer. You still have to open it, check the date, and confirm the page actually says what the answer claims. This lesson is the habit of doing that every time, and it opens the Professional level, where the standard is work you can hand to someone else.

The next lesson turns files into client documents. Research done right is where those documents get their facts.

## The lesson

### 1. Ask a decision question

Research without a decision is reading. Start by naming the decision. "Should I add online ordering before the fall semester starts?" "Should I switch from my current scheduling tool to a cheaper one?" "Do I need a permit to put a sign on the truck?"

Then turn the decision into the research question: the thing you would need to know to decide. "What do the main online ordering options for a single-location coffee shop cost per month and per order right now, and what hardware do they require?" That is a question the web can answer with dated pages.

Keep the question narrow enough to finish. "Everything about online ordering" never finishes. Three named options, two cost lines, one hardware question finishes today.

In the chat, make sure search is on. Look for the web search control in the message box tools, the globe icon in most layouts, or just say "use the web" the way the prompt does. For a deeper question that needs many sources, and when you can wait several minutes, the deep research option produces a longer cited report. Use it when the decision is worth the wait.

What good looks like: the question names the decision, the options, and the time frame, and you can say what answer would change your mind.

The mistake is asking an opinion question. "Is online ordering worth it?" gets you an essay. "What does it cost and what does it require?" gets you facts you can act on.

Example: a roofer in Marshall wants to know current registration requirements for roofing contractors in Texas. The decision is whether to register this quarter. Anything touching rules, have your attorney review it. The research still has to be current and sourced.

### 2. Prefer primary current sources

A primary source is the page that owns the fact. The vendor's own pricing page. The agency's own rules page. The manufacturer's own spec sheet. The company's own announcement. A secondary source is someone writing about it: a blog post, a comparison site, a news article, a forum thread.

Secondary sources are fine for finding out what exists. They are not fine for the number you are going to act on. Blogs go stale and comparison sites get paid.

The exact working prompt below asks for the source title, direct link, publication or update date, and what the source supports, for every material claim. Material means it would change your decision. It also asks for four buckets: verified facts, reasonable inferences, conflicting evidence, and unanswered questions. Keep those buckets separate in your head, because ChatGPT will happily blend an inference into a fact if you let it.

When the answer comes back, look at the citations. They show as source links in the text and a sources list at the end of the answer. Count how many are primary. If most of the material claims lean on a blog, ask again: "For claims one, three, and four, find the primary source page and give me the direct link and the date on that page."

What good looks like: every number you plan to use points to a page owned by whoever controls that number, with a date you can see.

The mistake is trusting a date because the answer states one. The date has to be on the page. If the page has no date, note that. An undated page is a weaker source.

Example: for the ordering question, the primary sources are the three vendors' pricing pages. A "best ordering apps of 2026" article is only a map.

### 3. Verify claims across the source trail

Now the part that makes this research and not a chat. Open every cited link. Every one.

For each source, log four things in the source verification log from the workbook: the claim, the link, the date shown on the page, and whether the page directly supports the claim. Directly means you can put your finger on the sentence or the number. Not "it is probably in there somewhere."

Three things happen. Some links support the claim: keep them. Some links are real but say something different: the claim gets corrected to what the page says. Some links do not support the claim at all, or the page is about something else, or the link is dead: the claim gets removed. Not softened. Removed.

Then check across sources. If two pages disagree, that goes in the conflicting evidence bucket, and you say which one is more authoritative and why. If a question came back unanswered, it stays listed as unanswered. Do not let a guess fill it.

Finish with the line the prompt asks for: the decision this research can support and the decision it cannot. "This supports comparing monthly cost across the three options. It does not tell me how many of my customers would order online, because there is no evidence for that."

What good looks like: a second person could follow your log and land on the same facts without talking to you.

The mistake is verifying two links and assuming the rest. The one you skip is the one that was wrong.

Example: the coffee shop owner opens six links. Five hold. One is a 2023 blog quoting an old fee. She removes the claim, searches the vendor page, logs the current number with the date, and moves on.

## Worked example

Kilgore Kettle Coffee is a fictional example. Jen owns the one shop, across from the college. Decision: add online ordering before the fall semester or wait a year. Research question: what do three online ordering options for a single-location shop cost per month and per order as of this month, and what hardware do they require.

She opens a chat in her project named "Online ordering research," confirms web search is on, and runs the exact working prompt with the question filled in.

Example output, with vendor names removed here:

```text
Verified facts:
1. Vendor A charges a monthly plan fee plus a per-order fee. Source: Vendor A pricing page, direct link, page shows an update date this quarter. Supports the monthly and per-order cost lines.
2. Vendor B has no monthly fee and a higher per-order fee. Source: Vendor B pricing page, direct link, dated this year.
Reasonable inference: Vendor B likely costs less below a certain order volume. This is math on the fees above, not a stated fact.
Conflicting evidence: a comparison article lists a different Vendor A fee than the pricing page. The pricing page is primary and more recent.
Unanswered: whether Vendor C requires its own tablet. The page does not say.
This research supports comparing monthly cost at a given order volume. It does not support predicting how many customers will order online.
```

Jen opens all six links. Five hold and have visible dates. The sixth is the comparison article. She logs it as conflicting and defers to the pricing page. She asks a follow-up for Vendor C's hardware requirement, gets a support page with the answer, opens it, and logs it.

Review against the pass standard. Material claims current: every page dated this year. Traceable to direct sources: yes, all primary except the one she demoted. Dated: yes. Separated from inference: the volume math is labeled as inference, not fact.

The one revision that made it better: the first answer had included "most shops see higher average tickets with online ordering." No source. She removed it. That kind of line is how a decision gets made on a hope.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Research this current question: [question]. Use the web. Prefer primary and authoritative sources. For each material claim, provide the source title, direct link, publication or update date, and what it supports. Separate verified facts, reasonable inferences, conflicting evidence, and unanswered questions. End with the decision this research can and cannot support.
```

## Guided practice

1. Write down one real decision you are facing and the research question that would settle it.
2. Open a chat in your project, turn on web search, and run the exact working prompt with the question filled in.
3. Open every cited link and log the claim, link, date on the page, and whether it directly supports the claim.
4. Remove every claim a source does not directly support, and re-ask for primary sources where a blog was cited.
5. Write the last two lines yourself: what this research supports and what it does not.

## Assignment and evidence

Research one real business decision, open every cited source, and remove any claim the source does not directly support.

Save the source material, the prompt, the first result, the final result, and your review notes.

## Pass standard

Material claims are current, traceable to direct sources, dated, and clearly separated from inference.

## Common mistakes

- Asking an opinion question. Fix: ask what it costs, what it requires, and by when, tied to a decision.
- Accepting a blog as the source for a number. Fix: ask for the primary page and the date on it.
- Trusting the date in the answer. Fix: find the date on the page itself, and note when there is none.
- Opening two links and assuming the rest. Fix: open every one and log it.
- Letting a hopeful line stay because it sounds right. Fix: no source, no claim.
