# CG L01: Set Up a Project That Remembers the Work

## What you will finish

Create one clean ChatGPT Project with instructions, reference files, and separate working chats that keep business context organized. When you are done you will have a Project in your ChatGPT sidebar with your business facts written into its instructions, one trusted file attached, and a chat named First Working Build sitting inside it, ready for the next lesson.

## Why this matters

Most people use ChatGPT like a search box. New chat, type a question, get something generic, close the tab. Then they tell me it does not work for their business.

Here is what that looks like. A roofer in Gilmer told me he tried ChatGPT for a Facebook post and it wrote him three paragraphs about "premium roofing solutions for discerning homeowners." Of course it did. It did not know he has run hail claims for fourteen years, that he answers his own phone, or that half his jobs come from Upshur County. He never told it. He would have had to retype all of that every single time, so he stopped.

A Project fixes that. It is a folder inside ChatGPT that holds your instructions and your files, and every chat you start inside it can use them. You set it up once. You stop repeating yourself. The output starts sounding like your business instead of a brochure.

This is lesson one because every lesson after it runs inside this Project. The content kit, the images, the research, the client documents, the capstone. If the Project is muddy, everything you build on it is muddy. Ten minutes here saves you hours later.

## The lesson

### 1. Name the project for one durable outcome

Open ChatGPT. In the left sidebar you will see Projects. Click New project. It asks for a name.

Do not call it Marketing. Do not call it Business Stuff. Name it for one outcome that will still make sense in six months. "Sabine Valley Plumbing: Customer Communication." "Kilgore Family Dental: Patient Emails and Posts." "Marshall Youth League: Board Reports."

One project, one job. If you have two jobs, make two projects. A project that writes your customer emails should not also be the project that writes your hiring ads, because those need different instructions and the instructions will fight each other.

The test: read the name out loud. You should know instantly what belongs inside it and what does not.

Next, open the project and find Instructions. Click it. This is where the durable context lives: who ChatGPT is acting as, who it is writing for, how you talk, which facts are approved, what it must never do, what a finished piece looks like, and what it should ask before it starts. That is seven parts, and the exact working prompt at the bottom of this lesson drafts all seven with you. Start a chat inside the project, paste the prompt with your business filled in, and answer its questions with real facts.

Then read every line of what it drafts. Cut anything that is not true. Next to each fact, note where it came from: "(source: price sheet, March 2026)." Paste the approved version into Instructions and save.

One more setting. Under Settings, then Personalization, there is Custom instructions. That applies to every chat you ever open, so keep it short and about you as a person: plain English, short sentences, no em dashes, ask before assuming. Business facts go in the Project, not there.

The mistake people make is pasting the draft instructions without reading them. ChatGPT will draft a confident line like "offers 24/7 emergency service" because plumbers often do. If you do not offer it, and you leave that line in, every email for the next year will promise it.

### 2. Add only trusted reference material

Inside the project, find Files and click Add files. Trusted means two things: you wrote it or approved it, and it is current.

The best first file is a one-page fact sheet. Services you actually offer. Service area by town. Hours. The office phone. Years in business. Prices you are willing to publish, or the sentence "estimates given on site." Two or three plain sentences about why people pick you, in your own words. Type it in Google Docs or Word, export it as a PDF, and name it so you can read the date: sabine-valley-fact-sheet-2026-03.pdf.

Other good files: your current website copy, a price list, a FAQ built from questions customers really ask.

Files to leave out: the 2019 flyer with old prices, a competitor's brochure, contracts, and any spreadsheet with customer names, phone numbers, or payment details. Drafting your communication needs facts about you. It does not need data about your customers, and the pass standard for this lesson says no unnecessary private information. Keep it out.

What good looks like: two or three files, each named for what it is and when it was true.

The mistake is uploading fifteen files just in case. Every extra file is another place an old price or a dead phone number can leak into a new draft. One approved fact sheet beats a folder of maybes.

Example: a gym owner in Tyler adds her fact sheet, her class schedule PDF, and nothing else. When the schedule changes, she deletes the old file and adds the new one the same day.

### 3. Open a separate chat for each deliverable

Now the part that keeps the project clean over time. Inside the project, start a new chat for each thing you are making. One chat for the spring drain special email. One for this month's Facebook posts. One for the FAQ rewrite. Every chat inside the project sees the instructions and the files, so you never repeat yourself. But each chat stays about one thing.

Rename each chat so the name is the deliverable. Click the three dots next to the chat in the sidebar and choose Rename. "Spring drain special email." "FAQ page rewrite." For this lesson, the first one is named First Working Build.

Why separate chats? Long chats drift. Forty messages in, ChatGPT starts blending your third draft of the email into the post you asked for. Short, single-purpose chats stay sharp. And when a deliverable is done, that chat is your record: the prompt, the drafts, the final. You can find it in a month.

Use First Working Build to test the setup. Ask for a two-sentence description of your business. Check every word against your fact sheet. If it invents something, fix the instructions, not the output. Add the line "Do not claim services not listed in the fact sheet" and run it again.

Some plans show a memory setting inside the project. If yours does, keep it set so the project uses only its own chats, so your personal chats do not bleed into your business writing.

The mistake is one endless chat where everything happens. The fix is simple. New chat per deliverable, renamed, and when one gets long, start fresh and paste in only the final approved text.

## Worked example

Sabine Valley Plumbing in Marshall is a fictional example. Dale runs it. Two trucks, in business since 2011, repipes, water heaters, drain cleaning, slab leak detection. Marshall, Hallsville, and Longview. Monday through Friday, seven to six. No after-hours service.

Dale names the project "Sabine Valley Plumbing: Customer Communication." He starts a chat inside it and pastes the exact working prompt with "[business or outcome]" replaced by "Sabine Valley Plumbing customer emails, texts, and Facebook posts."

ChatGPT asks six questions: what he does, where, who calls him, how he talks, what he will never promise, and what a finished piece should look like. He answers from his fact sheet in plain sentences. It drafts the instructions.

Example output:

```text
Role: You write customer-facing emails, texts, and Facebook posts for Sabine Valley Plumbing, Marshall, Texas.
Audience: Homeowners in Marshall, Hallsville, and Longview, mostly 35 to 70, calling about a problem that already exists.
Voice: Plain, calm, direct. Short sentences. No exclamation points. No slang.
Approved facts (source: fact sheet, March 2026): in business since 2011, two trucks, Mon to Fri 7 to 6, services listed below.
Boundaries: Do not promise same-day, after-hours, or 24/7 service. Do not quote prices. Say "estimate on site."
Before acting: Ask who the reader is, what one action they should take, and whether there is a deadline.
```

Dale reviews it against the pass standard. Single purpose: yes. Source-labeled context: he added the "(source: fact sheet, March 2026)" tag himself on the second pass. Clear boundaries: yes. Unnecessary private information: the draft had pulled in his personal cell number from the conversation. He swapped it for the office line.

The one revision that made it better: the first draft's "Before acting" line just said "ask clarifying questions if unsure." That is useless. He replaced it with the three specific questions you see above. Now every chat in the project opens by asking who, what action, and when.

He pastes the approved instructions in, adds the fact sheet PDF, opens a chat named First Working Build, and asks for a two-sentence description. It comes back clean.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Help me set up a ChatGPT Project for [business or outcome]. Ask me for the minimum information needed, then draft project instructions covering role, audience, voice, approved facts, boundaries, output standards, and what to ask before acting. Do not invent business facts.
```

## Guided practice

1. Open ChatGPT, click Projects in the sidebar, click New project, and name it for one outcome.
2. Start a chat inside the project, paste the exact working prompt with your business filled in, and answer every question with real facts.
3. Read the drafted instructions line by line, delete anything untrue, tag each fact with its source, and paste the result into Instructions.
4. Add one trusted file, ideally a dated one-page fact sheet.
5. Rename the chat First Working Build, ask for a two-sentence description of your business, and check every word against the file.

## Assignment and evidence

Create the project, paste the approved instructions, add one trusted file, and open a chat named First Working Build.

Save the source material, the prompt, the first result, the final result, and your review notes.

## Pass standard

The project has a single purpose, source-labeled context, clear boundaries, and no private information that is unnecessary for the work.

## Common mistakes

- Naming the project after a tool or a department. Fix: name it for the outcome, and split it if there are two.
- Pasting the drafted instructions without reading them. Fix: read every line, cut what is not true, tag each fact with its source.
- Uploading customer lists or contracts. Fix: drafting needs facts about you, not data about your customers.
- Running everything in one long chat. Fix: one chat per deliverable, renamed to match.
- Blaming the tool when it invents a service. Fix: add the boundary that stops it and run the test again.
