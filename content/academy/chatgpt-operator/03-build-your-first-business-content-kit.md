# CG L03: Build Your First Business Content Kit

## What you will finish

Turn one approved business fact set into a coordinated social post, email, short video script, and call to action. When you are done you will have one saved source brief and five finished pieces that all say the same true thing, each shaped for its channel, in one document you can share.

## Why this matters

Here is how most small business content gets made. Monday, a Facebook post written on the phone in the truck. Wednesday, an email from a template that still has last year's price. Friday, a video shot in the parking lot that mentions a deal nobody else has heard of. Three pieces, three different stories, one confused customer.

A gym owner in Tyler showed me her month. The post said the fall program started the 15th. The email said the 22nd. She had changed the date and updated one, not the other. Two people showed up on the wrong day.

The fix is to write the facts once, approve them once, and make every piece from that one page. That page is the source brief. It is the most boring document in this course and the one that saves you the most embarrassment.

This is the first deliverable lesson, and it closes the Beginner level. Lessons one and two gave you the project and the prompt frame. Now you use both to ship something a customer will actually see.

## The lesson

### 1. Approve one source brief

The source brief is one page. Type it yourself, in your project chat or in a document. It has six parts.

The one idea. What is this content about? One thing. A fall program, a spring drain special, a new location, a hiring push. Not three things.

The facts. What, who it is for, when, where, and how to respond. If you are publishing a price, the price. If there is a limit, the real limit. Every fact tagged with where it came from, the way you learned in lesson two.

The next action. One. Call this number. Text this word to this number. Click this link. Not "call, text, message, or stop by." Pick one and the whole kit points at it.

The voice. Three words and one sample sentence you actually said. "Plain, warm, no shouting. Example: 'We start at six because that is when most of you can make it.'"

The off-limits list. Claims you will not make. No results promises, no fake urgency, no testimonials you do not have written permission to use.

Approved means you read it slowly and every fact is true today. Then save it with a name you can find: brief-fall-strength-program-2026-09. Add it to the project files or paste it into the chat.

What good looks like: someone else could make the content from this page without asking you anything.

The mistake is skipping the brief and "just telling ChatGPT about it" in the prompt. Then the facts live in a chat you will never find again, and next month's kit starts from memory.

### 2. Adapt the idea to each channel

The exact working prompt below turns the brief into five pieces in one pass. One Facebook post, one Instagram caption, one 90-word email, one 30-second direct-to-camera script, and one call to action that is identical across all five.

Here is what each channel wants, so you can judge what comes back.

The Facebook post reads like you talking to people who already know you. Sixty to 120 words. Opens with the situation, not the offer. Ends on the next action.

The Instagram caption is shorter, broken into short lines so it reads on a phone, with three to five local hashtags at most. Same facts.

The email has a subject line under about 45 characters and a 90-word body. One link or one phone number. Your name at the bottom, not "The Team."

The 30-second script is 75 to 85 words spoken. First line is the hook, said the way you would say it to a camera in your shop. Middle is the offer. Last line is the action. No stage directions you will not follow.

The call to action is one sentence, word for word the same in all five. "Text START to 903-555-0142 to hold a spot."

Run the prompt in a new chat inside your project. Name the chat for the kit. Paste the brief where the prompt says [paste], and describe the voice where it says [describe] using your three words and the sample sentence.

The mistake is letting each piece grow its own facts. The email gets a bonus, the post gets a different date, the script adds "limited time." One brief, one prompt, one pass keeps them aligned.

Example: a church in Marshall runs the prompt for a fall food drive. Same date, same drop-off address, same phone number in all five pieces, because they all came from one page.

### 3. Run a consistency review

Do not post anything yet. Put all five pieces in one document and read them in a row. You are checking two things.

Same truth. Same date, same price, same phone number, same limit, same next action in all five. Circle anything that appears in one piece and not in the brief. That is an invented claim, and it goes.

Channel fit. Does the Facebook post read as a wall of text? Does the caption break into lines? Is the subject line too long? Would you actually say the script out loud, or does it sound like a commercial?

Then make one revision request, and make it targeted. "Revise only the script. It is 110 words. Cut to 80, keep the phone number, end on the text instruction." Do not say "make it better." Say what is wrong and what fixed looks like.

Read the revised piece once more against the brief. Save the final brief and the five outputs together in one document. That document is your evidence for this lesson.

What good looks like: a customer who sees the post on Tuesday and the email on Thursday hears one story.

The mistake is reviewing one piece at a time. The mismatches only show up when they sit next to each other.

Example: a detailer in Kilgore reviews her kit and finds the email says "this week only" and the brief says nothing about a deadline. She cuts it. Her customers do not get a fake countdown, and she does not have to explain it next week.

## Worked example

Iron Oak Fitness in Tyler is a fictional example. Dana owns it. Her brief: a six-week small-group strength program, starts September 15, mornings at 6, eight spots, $180 for the six weeks (source: her fall price sheet), for adults who have never lifted with a coach. Next action: text START to 903-555-0142 to hold a spot. Voice: plain, encouraging, no shouting. Off-limits: no results promises, no before-and-after claims, no urgency beyond the real eight spots.

She opens a chat in her project named "Fall strength kit," pastes the exact working prompt, drops the brief into [paste], and writes "plain, encouraging, no exclamation points, sounds like a coach talking after class" in [describe].

Example output:

```text
Facebook post: New to lifting and want a coach in the room? Our six-week small-group strength program starts September 15, mornings at 6. Eight people, one coach, $180 for the six weeks. Text START to 903-555-0142 to hold a spot.
Instagram caption: Six weeks. Eight spots. One coach. Starts Sept 15 at 6 a.m. Spots going fast. Text START to 903-555-0142 to hold a spot. #TylerTX #IronOakFitness
Email subject: Six-week strength program starts Sept 15
Script opening: If you have been meaning to start lifting but did not want to figure it out alone, this one is for you.
Call to action: Text START to 903-555-0142 to hold a spot.
```

Review against the pass standard. All five match the same facts and next action? The date, the price, the number, and the eight spots matched in every piece. But the caption had added "spots going fast," which is urgency that was not in the brief. And the email body said "you will feel stronger in two weeks," which is a results promise. Two circles.

The one revision: "Revise only the Instagram caption and the email. Remove 'spots going fast' and the two-week line. Keep everything else." It came back clean. She saved the brief and the five pieces in one Google Doc named "Iron Oak fall strength kit, final," set it to anyone with the link can view, and that is what she submits.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Using only this approved business brief: [paste], create a content kit with one Facebook post, one Instagram caption, one 90-word email, one 30-second direct-to-camera script, and one consistent call to action. Keep the voice [describe]. Do not add claims, statistics, testimonials, or urgency that are not in the brief.
```

## Pass standard

All five pieces match the same facts and next action while fitting their individual channels.

## Common mistakes

- Skipping the brief and describing the offer in the prompt. Fix: write the one page first, approve it, then prompt from it.
- Three different next actions across the kit. Fix: pick one and paste it word for word into every piece.
- Accepting urgency ChatGPT added. Fix: if the deadline or limit is not in the brief, cut it.
- Reviewing pieces one at a time. Fix: put all five in one document and read them in a row.
- Asking for "make it better." Fix: name the piece, the defect, and the target.
