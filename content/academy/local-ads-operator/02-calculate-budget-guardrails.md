# AD L02: Calculate Budget Guardrails

## What you will finish

Set a test budget and stop thresholds from economics and capacity. When you are done you will have a one-page budget sheet with a conservative, base, and strong case, a daily cap, a total cap, and stop rules written down before the first dollar goes out.

## Why this matters

Most owners set an ad budget the way they set a thermostat. Whatever feels comfortable. A gym in Longview spends $900 in a month "to see what happens," and at the end of the month nobody can say what came back. There was no rule to stop, so nobody stopped.

I made the opposite mistake with my own money. I turned off an ad after nineteen cents of spend. Six impressions. Four people reached. It had not failed. It had not run. Both mistakes come from the same place: no rule written down before the money moved.

The budget has to come from what a job is worth to you, not from what feels safe. A water heater install and a $40 oil change cannot afford the same lead. Your sheet says what you can pay, what you will spend to find out, and when you stop.

Bring the campaign brief from lesson one. The offer and the capacity number on it are the inputs here, and the caps you set now are what lesson seven enforces and lesson eight reads against.

## The lesson

### 1. Estimate contribution margin

Start with the average sale for the one offer on your brief. Pull the last ten invoices for that job and average them. Not your best job. The average.

Then figure what is left after the job is done. Parts, the labor you pay someone else, fuel, the disposal fee, the permit. That is contribution. It is not net profit after your salary and the shop rent. It is the money one more job adds to the pile.

Say the water heater job averages $1,800. Materials $700, tech labor $350, fuel and permit $50. Contribution is $700. Every number in this lesson is an example like that one. Yours will be different, and that is the point of doing it.

Now close rate. Of the qualified leads for this job, how many become a sale? Look at last month's calls if you logged them. If you did not, and most people did not, use three cases: conservative one in five, base one in three, strong one in two. Never one number. One number is a guess dressed up as a fact.

What good looks like: the average sale, the contribution, and three close rates, each with a note that says "from invoices," "from last month's calls," or "assumption."

The mistake: using revenue. An $1,800 sale is not $1,800 you can spend to get it. I watch owners run that math and wonder why the month ended in the red with a full calendar.

### 2. Choose an affordable acquisition range

Break-even lead cost is contribution times close rate. In the example, $700 times one in three is about $233. That is the most you could pay for one qualified lead and make exactly nothing. Run it for all three cases: $140 conservative, $233 base, $350 strong.

Your target is a fraction of break-even, because the campaign has to leave money for the business. I use a third as a starting point. The base case target lands around $78 per qualified lead.

Now hear this part clearly. Those numbers describe what you can afford. They do not predict what Meta will charge you. Nobody knows that until you run. I will not tell you your cost per lead, and anyone who tells you theirs as a promise is selling something. The sheet is an affordability check, not a forecast.

Add payback. If you get paid when the job is done, payback is days and the test budget only has to float a week or two. If you invoice net 30, the test budget has to cover a month before anything comes back. Write which one you are.

Then capacity, from the brief. Six installs a week at a one in three close is about 18 qualified leads a week. Past that you are paying for leads you cannot serve, and a lead you cannot serve turns into a one star review. Cap it there.

If you want to see what one missed lead costs in this offer, run the missed call calculator at /tools/missed-call-calculator. There are 86 free calculators at /tools. The missed call number belongs on the sheet, because follow-up speed changes close rate more than any ad setting does.

What good looks like: three break-even numbers, three targets, a payback line, and a weekly lead ceiling from capacity.

The mistake: treating the strong case as the plan. Plan on conservative. Be glad when base shows up.

### 3. Cap the learning test

Two caps and a set of stop rules. Daily cap and total cap.

The daily cap comes from the learning read. In my account, the first honest look at an offer needs 48 to 72 hours of delivery, or roughly $70 to $105 of spend, before I change anything structural. So daily budget times three days is the minimum you will spend to learn anything at all. At $35 a day that is $105. If that number scares you, run fewer days at the same daily budget, not more days at $5.

Do not split it. Eight ad sets at $5 a day is $40 a day that teaches you nothing, because none of them ever gets enough delivery to read. One ad set, one budget.

The total cap for the test: enough to buy roughly five to ten qualified leads at your target cost, or two weeks, whichever comes first. In the example, $500. If the target is wrong, and it might be, $500 tells you that without hurting the business.

Then the stop rules, written as sentences a spouse could enforce:

Stop when spend reaches $500 with fewer than three qualified leads.
Do not change anything before 72 hours unless something is broken, like a wrong link or a wrong price.
Pause if 40 people click and nobody completes the form. That is a form problem, not a spend problem.
Do not raise the daily budget more than 20 percent in a week.

The nineteen cent ad broke the second rule. Turning it off was not a decision. It was a reflex, and it cost me the only untested creative I had that week. Write the rule so you cannot flinch.

Put all of it on one page. Assumptions at the top, the math in the middle, caps and stop rules at the bottom. Every number in the middle points at an assumption at the top. That is the pass standard, and it is what lets you fix the sheet in week two instead of arguing about it.

What good looks like: one page, and a stranger could find where every number came from in under a minute.

The mistake: "let's see how it goes" with no number, or its cousin, moving the budget every morning.

## Worked example

Rabbit Creek Plumbing in Kilgore is a fictional example. Marisol pulled ten water heater invoices and counted last month's water heater calls before she opened the prompt.

Her inputs: average sale $1,780. Materials and paid labor $1,090, so contribution $690. Last month 4 of 11 qualified calls became installs, so base case one in three, conservative one in five, strong one in two. Capacity six installs a week. Paid on completion. Test budget available $500.

She ran the exact working prompt below with "water heater replacement" in the bracket and those numbers pasted above it.

Example output:

```text
Assumptions: average sale $1,780; contribution $690; close rate 20 / 33 / 50 percent; capacity 6 installs per week; payback at completion.
Break-even qualified lead cost: $138 conservative, $230 base, $345 strong.
Target qualified lead cost (one third of break-even): $46 / $77 / $115.
Daily cap: $35. Minimum learning spend: $105 over 72 hours.
Total cap: $500 or 14 days, whichever comes first.
Stop rules: stop at $500 with under 3 qualified leads; pause at 40 clicks and 0 form completions; no structural change before 72 hours.
Expected leads at $500: about 11.
```

Review against the pass standard. Does every number trace to an assumption? Yes, until the last line. "Expected leads: about 11" divides the total cap by the conservative target and calls it a result. That is a forecast wearing a plain shirt, and it uses the lowest number on the sheet to do it. Does spending have a fixed cap and stop rule? Yes, both.

The one revision: the last line became "At the base target, $500 buys about six qualified leads. That is an affordability check, not a prediction. Actual cost is unknown until the test runs." Marisol also wrote next to the strong case: "not for planning until it shows up in real data."

## Exact working prompt

```text
Build a budget model for [offer] using average sale, gross margin, close rate, capacity, target payback, and available test budget. Show assumptions, break-even lead cost, target lead cost, daily cap, total cap, and stop rules. Do not claim forecasts are guaranteed.
```

## Guided practice

1. Pull the last ten invoices for the offer on your brief and write the average sale and the contribution.
2. Write three close rates, conservative, base, and strong, and note which one has real data behind it.
3. Multiply contribution by each close rate for three break-even lead costs, then take a third of each for targets.
4. Set a daily cap and a total cap, and check that daily cap times three lands near $70 to $105.
5. Write four stop rules as plain sentences, then run the exact prompt with everything pasted in.

## Assignment and evidence

Complete the budget sheet with conservative, base, and strong cases.

Save the source material, the prompt, the first result, the final result, and your review notes.

## Pass standard

Every budget number traces to an assumption and spending has a fixed cap and stop rule.

## Common mistakes

- Using revenue instead of contribution. Fix: subtract parts, paid labor, fuel, and permits before you do anything else.
- One close rate. Fix: three cases, and label which one has data.
- Splitting a small budget across many ad sets. Fix: one ad set, one budget, until it has taught you something.
- No stop rule, or a stop rule nobody wrote down. Fix: sentences on the sheet, with numbers, before launch.
- Treating the sheet as a forecast. Fix: label it an affordability check and let the campaign supply the real cost.
