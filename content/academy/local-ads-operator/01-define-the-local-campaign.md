# AD L01: Define the Local Campaign

## What you will finish

Connect one audience, geography, offer, action, and business constraint. When you are done you will have a one-page campaign brief that says who the ad is for, where it runs, what it sells, what the viewer does next, and how many of those jobs your business can actually take next week.

## Why this matters

I ran an ad set called "Higher Intent" for two days before I opened the settings and read them. There was no targeting in it. None. It was running to every adult within 40 miles of Longview, which Meta estimated at somewhere between 577,000 and 679,000 people. The name said one thing. The setup said another. That is what happens when the brief lives in your head instead of on paper.

Here is what it looks like for a local business. A roofer in Gilmer runs "free roof inspections" to the whole ArkLaTex the week after a hail storm. The phone lights up. Half the calls are from an hour and a half away, and he cannot get there for two weeks. The calls he could serve get buried under the ones he cannot. He paid for every one of them.

The brief is what stops that. One area. One offer. One action. One honest number for what you can handle next week.

Everything after this lesson depends on it. You cannot do the budget math in lesson two until you know the one offer and the one area. You cannot write ad angles in lesson three until you know who is looking and what they are looking for.

## The lesson

### 1. Choose one service area

Open Google Maps. Drop a pin on your shop, or on your house if you run out of a truck. Now draw the circle you would actually drive for this job at this price. Not the circle you would drive for a $10,000 job. The circle for this offer.

Say it out loud in one breath. "Kilgore and 20 miles." "Tyler city limits plus Whitehouse." If it takes a paragraph, it is too big.

In Meta you set this with a pin and a radius, or a list of cities, and you can add exclusions. Use the exclusions. If you will not drive to Tyler in five o'clock traffic for a $300 job, exclude Tyler. Meta shows an estimated audience size as you draw. Write that number on the brief.

What good looks like: a radius you can say in one breath, a written exclusion list, and the audience number next to it.

The mistake: "East Texas." That is a region, not a service area. Forty miles around Longview is well over half a million adults, retirees and shift workers and kids who turned 18 last week. The opposite mistake is a five mile circle in a small town. That might be 4,000 adults, and Meta will show the same people your ad ten times in a week and charge you for every showing.

Example: a mobile detailer in Marshall. He will drive 25 minutes for a $250 detail. That is 15 miles, not Shreveport. Marshall, Hallsville, Jefferson, and the lake. Done.

### 2. Use one offer

If you finished The Offer Engine, bring the offer from OE L03. If you did not, pick the one job you want more of and write it so a stranger can say yes without a meeting. What it is, what it costs or how it is scoped, and where it stops.

One offer per campaign. Not "we do everything." An ad that lists nine services sells none of them.

This is the biggest thing I learned spending my own money. In my account, a free build offer ran on broad targeting and pulled 12 leads across three forms. A paid diagnostic ran on the same targeting, same area, same video, and pulled zero leads on 71 clicks and $110. Nothing changed except the offer. The offer was the variable, not the targeting. When someone tells you to fix your audience, look at your offer first.

Write the offer once and make every surface copy it. The ad, the form, the page, the text, the email. My ad promised a free look at what to fix first. My form opened with a price ladder that topped out at "$7,500 and up." Nobody had earned the right to ask that yet. Zero leads.

Seasonality and capacity go on the brief right here. Water heaters fail in the January cold snap. AC calls stack up in June. Roofers get slammed after hail. Then the honest number: how many of these jobs can you take next week without hurting the customers you already have. Two trucks and a full service schedule might mean six water heaters a week. Write six. That number caps your budget in lesson two.

What good looks like: one sentence with a price or a scope and a boundary, and a capacity number a crew lead would agree with.

The mistake: the offer drifts between surfaces. The ad says one thing, the form asks for another, the page sells a third.

### 3. Define a qualified result

Decide what counts before you spend a dollar. "Lead" is not a result. Meta will report leads. Some of them are a teenager who tapped the wrong thing. One of mine had a Houston area code. Your brief needs a definition a human can apply on the phone.

For a plumber it might read: a homeowner inside the area, with a problem inside this offer, who gave a working phone number and answered or replied. That is a qualified lead. An appointment is better. A paid job is the truth.

Then the one action the ad asks for. In my account I run instant form lead ads only. If I spend money I collect a name, a phone, and an email so I can call the person and follow up. I tested link ads to a page and got 210 landing page views and zero contacts. Your action might be a form, a call button, or a booking page. Pick one. Write it down.

Do not measure clicks. Seventy one people clicked my ad and nobody filled out the form. If I had been watching clicks I would have been thrilled all the way to a zero.

One note on platforms. Meta, meaning Facebook and Instagram, is where I start, because I can reach people before they search and the cost to test is low. Google Local Services Ads and Google Search catch people already typing "water heater repair Kilgore." Higher intent, different rules, usually more budget to learn. Put your platform on the brief. Start with one.

Last field: constraints. What you will not say and will not do. No before and after claims without proof. No "cheapest." No promises of results. If you do not know your close rate, write "unknown." The prompt asks the model to flag missing evidence, so let it. A brief with three honest unknowns beats one with nine invented numbers.

What good looks like: your office person reads the qualified lead line and can sort yesterday's calls with it.

The mistake: measuring what the platform hands you instead of what the business needs.

## Worked example

Rabbit Creek Plumbing in Kilgore is a fictional example. Owner Marisol, two trucks, one office person who answers the phone from 7 to 7.

She ran the lesson prompt with her notes pasted in: offer is water heater replacement with a quote by phone within 24 hours. Area is Kilgore and 20 miles. Exclude Tyler. Capacity is six water heater installs a week on top of service calls. Busy season is December through February. Qualified lead is a homeowner in the area with a failing or failed water heater who gives a working phone number. Primary action is an instant form. Close rate unknown.

"Create a local campaign brief for Rabbit Creek Plumbing. Include offer, buyer, service area, exclusions, seasonality, capacity, qualified lead definition, landing page, primary action, and constraints. Flag missing evidence."

Example output:

```text
Offer: water heater replacement, same-day install, quote by phone within 24 hours.
Buyer: homeowner inside Kilgore plus 20 miles, tank over 10 years old or already failed.
Service area: Kilgore pin, 20 mile radius. Exclusions: Tyler, commercial, property managers over 5 units.
Seasonality: peak December to February. Capacity: 6 installs per week; pause ads when the calendar is full 5 business days out.
Qualified lead: in area, residential, working phone, answered or replied within 2 attempts.
Primary action: instant form. Landing page: thank-you page with callback window.
Missing evidence: close rate unknown, average ticket not provided, no permission-cleared job photos on file.
```

Review against the pass standard. Can the business fulfill the promoted offer in the selected area? Not as written. "Same-day install" is a promise Marisol cannot keep when both trucks are booked, which is most of January. Can it define a qualified lead? Yes, and the two-attempt rule is something the office person can actually apply.

The one revision: same-day became "quote within 24 hours, install within 3 business days." That is a promise she keeps on her worst week. The three flagged unknowns went on her list for lesson two: pull the last ten water heater invoices, count last month's calls, and get a homeowner's OK on two job photos.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Create a local campaign brief for [business]. Include offer, buyer, service area, exclusions, seasonality, capacity, qualified lead definition, landing page, primary action, and constraints. Flag missing evidence.
```

## Guided practice

1. Open Google Maps, drop a pin, draw the circle you would drive for this offer, and write it as one sentence with exclusions.
2. Write the one offer in one sentence with its price or scope and where it stops.
3. Write the number of these jobs you can take next week without hurting current customers.
4. Write the qualified lead definition so your office person could apply it on the phone.
5. Run the exact prompt with all of that pasted in, and keep every missing evidence flag it returns.

## Pass standard

The business can fulfill the promoted offer in the selected area and define a qualified lead.

## Common mistakes

- Naming the ad set for an intent the settings do not produce. Fix: read every setting before you name anything.
- Running a region instead of a service area. Fix: the circle you would drive for this job at this price, with exclusions.
- Three offers in one ad. Fix: one offer per campaign, written once, copied to every surface.
- Calling every form fill a lead. Fix: a written qualified lead definition a human applies.
- Making up the close rate to fill the box. Fix: write unknown and go count last month's calls.
