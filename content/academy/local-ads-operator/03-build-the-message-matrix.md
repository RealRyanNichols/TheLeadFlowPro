# AD L03: Build the Message Matrix

## What you will finish

Create distinct ad angles from real problems, triggers, proof, and objections. When you are done you will have a five-row matrix in your workbook, three angles picked from it, and one ad brief for each that a person with a phone could shoot from tomorrow.

## Why this matters

Most local ads say the same thing. Family owned. Licensed and insured. Call today. That is not an angle. That is a business card with a budget behind it.

An angle is the reason a person stops scrolling. My best ad opened with a question about the viewer: what do you pay every month for the tools that run your business? My weakest opened with a list of things I had built. Good footage, clean sound, five proof cards, and it ran at more than five times the cost per click. It was about me. The winner was about them.

Here is the other reason. If you run five ads that are the same idea in five fonts and one wins, you learned nothing. If you run five different ideas and one wins, you learned what your buyers respond to. That is worth more than the leads it produced.

Bring the brief from lesson one. The offer on it is the thing every angle has to sell. The proof column you build here is what lesson four is not allowed to shoot without.

## The lesson

### 1. Collect buyer language

You do not write the angles. Your customers already did. Your job is to go find what they said.

Open your call log. If you use Quo or any business phone that records, read the transcripts of the last ten calls about this offer. Open your Google reviews, and your two biggest competitors' reviews, especially the one star ones. Scroll the last twenty texts from customers. Sit in the community Facebook group for your town for fifteen minutes.

Write down exact phrases. Not the gist. The words. "It went out again." "Y'all come out to Hallsville?" "How soon can somebody get here." "I called three places and nobody called back." "Is that the real price or is there going to be more."

Then the triggers. The moment somebody starts looking. The cold snap. The pilot light. Water on the garage floor. Hail on the news. The kid's first cavity. A wedding in six weeks. The trigger is where the ad meets them.

What good looks like: twenty to thirty raw lines, each labeled with where it came from, and five triggers. Then paste the lines into ChatGPT or Claude with the prompt. Do not ask it to invent buyer language. It will, and it will sound like every other ad in the feed.

The mistake: writing what you would say about yourself. Nobody is scrolling Facebook hoping to learn that you are licensed.

Example: a dentist in Tyler reads her last month of reviews and finds the same line four times: "they did not make me feel bad about how long it had been." That is an angle. It was sitting in her reviews the whole time.

### 2. Separate angle from format

An angle is the idea. The missed call. The monthly rent. The 9 PM Friday when nobody answers. The old tank in the garage that is twelve years old.

A format is how it is delivered. A phone video. A still picture. A carousel. A text-only Search ad. Keep them in separate columns, because you will test them separately.

I learned this the expensive way. Two of my four ads were video, and they sat at opposite ends of the results table. "Video wins" was the wrong read. The video that won had motion through a real place people recognize, other people on camera, and a question in the first three seconds. The video that lost was me talking against a lake. Same format. Different angle, different execution.

So the matrix has rows for angles and columns for the parts. Buyer trigger. Problem. Promise. Proof available. Limitation. Hook. Body. Call to action. Visual idea. Five rows, and each row has to be materially different: a different trigger or a different objection, not the same promise with a new adjective.

Example, five for a plumber. One, the cold shower this morning. Two, the "nobody called me back" objection. Three, the price question answered before they ask it. Four, the twelve year old tank that has not failed yet. Five, the landlord with three rentals who wants one number to call.

What good looks like: read the trigger column top to bottom and every line is a different moment.

The mistake: five rows that all say "we are fast." If two rows share a trigger, merge them and go find another.

### 3. Match claims to proof

Every promise in the matrix needs a proof you can put on a screen. A photo of your truck at a real job, with the homeowner's OK. A review shown the way the reviewer wrote it, with the name they used. Your license number. A count you can back with invoices. "We replaced 140 water heaters last year" is a claim only if the invoices say 140.

If you cannot prove it, cut the claim and keep the feeling. I hold to that on every ad I write for a client. The emotional line stays. The figure goes unless it is documented.

The limitation column matters more than people think. What the offer does not include. Commercial, not this offer. Tankless, quoted separately. Outside the area, no. Putting the boundary in the ad keeps the wrong people from clicking, and you pay for every click.

Some things never go in the matrix. No before and after claims unless verified and allowed. No promises of results. No "cheapest in town" unless you priced every competitor this week. Nothing about a person's health, credit, or housing situation, which Meta's special ad categories cover. If your business touches health, finance, or housing, have your attorney review the claims once and move on.

One note for Google. The same matrix feeds a Search campaign. The hook becomes a headline. The proof becomes the review extension. The limitation becomes a negative keyword. Build the matrix once and both platforms eat from it.

Then pick three. Not the three you like. The three with the strongest trigger and the proof already in hand. Write one ad brief per angle: the row from the matrix, the proof file named, the format you will shoot first. That is what you carry into lesson four.

What good looks like: every promise in the three briefs has a file name next to it.

The mistake: keeping a claim because it sounds good. Sounds good is not a proof file.

## Worked example

Rabbit Creek Plumbing in Kilgore is a fictional example. Marisol pulled 24 lines from Quo transcripts, her Google reviews, and a competitor's one star reviews. A few of them: "I called at 6 and nobody picked up." "Is that the real price." "The last guy said Tuesday and showed up Friday." "It's making that noise again." "Do y'all do Overton."

She pasted all 24 lines and the offer from her brief above the prompt.

"Create a message matrix for water heater replacement, quote by phone within 24 hours, Kilgore and 20 miles. Produce five angles. For each include buyer trigger, problem, promise, proof available, limitation, hook, body, call to action, and visual idea. Remove claims that lack evidence."

Example output:

```text
Angle 2. Trigger: called a plumber after hours, no answer. Problem: they call the next name on the list. Promise: a real person calls you back within the hour, 7 to 7. Proof: office callback log, last 30 days. Limitation: after 7 PM, first thing next morning. Hook: "Who called you back last time your water heater quit?" Visual: office phone ringing, Marisol picking up.
Angle 3. Trigger: worried the quote will grow once the truck arrives. Problem: surprise charges. Promise: lowest price in Kilgore. Proof: none on file. Limitation: none listed. Hook: "Lowest price in Kilgore." Visual: price tag.
```

Review against the pass standard. Are the angles materially different? Yes. Angle 2 is about being reached and angle 3 is about price, and they come from different triggers. Is every claim supportable? Angle 3 is not. "Lowest price in Kilgore" has no proof, Marisol has not priced her competitors, and the prompt was supposed to remove it.

The one revision: angle 3's promise became "the price we say on the phone is the price on the invoice," which her office backs with a written policy and the last 30 invoices. The hook became "Is that the real price? Yes. Here is how." The limitation column got filled in: "excludes code repairs found on site, quoted before any work starts." Angles 1, 2, and 3 became her three ad briefs.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Create a message matrix for [offer]. Produce five angles. For each include buyer trigger, problem, promise, proof available, limitation, hook, body, call to action, and visual idea. Remove claims that lack evidence.
```

## Guided practice

1. Read ten call transcripts or reviews and write twenty exact phrases with their source next to each.
2. Write five triggers, the moments your buyer starts looking.
3. Run the exact prompt with the phrases and your offer pasted above it.
4. Go down the proof column and strike every claim you cannot put on a screen.
5. Pick the three angles with the strongest trigger and proof in hand, and write one ad brief for each.

## Pass standard

Each angle is materially different and every claim is supportable.

## Common mistakes

- Writing angles from your own head. Fix: twenty exact customer phrases first, then the prompt.
- Five angles with one trigger. Fix: merge duplicates and find a different moment.
- Confusing format with angle. Fix: separate columns, and test angles inside one format first.
- Keeping a claim because it sounds good. Fix: no proof file, no claim. Keep the feeling, drop the figure.
- Skipping the limitation column. Fix: write what the offer does not include so the wrong people do not click.
