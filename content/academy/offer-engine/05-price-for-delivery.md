# OE L05: Price for Delivery

## What you will finish

Set a price floor from real delivery cost, risk, support, and margin. When you are done you will have a completed price-floor worksheet with the math visible, and two numbers chosen: a founding price for your first few buyers and a regular price after that.

## Why this matters

The $150 job that took six hours. Then two callbacks. Then a text on Sunday night. Then a two-star review anyway, because the buyer thought $150 bought a lot more than it did. Nobody set out to lose money on that job. It was priced on a guess and a fear of hearing "too much."

Most owners I meet price one of two ways. They look at what the guy down the road charges, or they pick a number that feels safe. Neither one knows what it costs you to deliver. So the price is high enough to lose a few bids and low enough to lose money on the ones you win. That is the worst of both.

A price floor fixes the bottom. It is the number below which you are paying to work. You may price above it for lots of reasons. You never go below it, and knowing exactly why you never go below it is what lets you say the number without flinching.

Bring the scope card from lesson three and the proof inventory from lesson four. The scope tells you the hours. The proof tells you how much risk you are carrying. Price comes after both, and before objections, because price is the first objection you will hear.

## The lesson

### 1. Calculate labor and hard cost

Start with what the scope card actually takes to deliver. Not what you hope. What it took last time.

How to do it: go deliverable by deliverable. Write the hours for each. Multiply by an hourly value that includes what it really costs to have that person working: wages plus payroll taxes plus insurance. If it is you, use the rate you would have to pay someone competent to replace you. Then list hard costs: materials, fuel, software seats, dump fees, subcontractors, printing.

What good looks like: a short table on the workbook Pricing math page. "Schedule: 2 hours. Weekly visits: 22 crew hours per month. Photo log: inside the visit. Fuel and equipment reserve: $80 per month."

The mistake: using base wages as the hourly value. A crew member at $18 an hour costs closer to $24 to $26 with taxes and workers' comp. Price on $18 and you lose every hour. And pricing your own time at zero is the most common mistake in this whole course.

Example: Caddo Plumbing, Longview, fictional. Water heater replacement. Labor: two techs, 3.5 hours each, seven hours at a loaded rate of $38, so $266. Hard costs: the unit, fittings, permit fee, and disposal, $780 at cost. That is $1,046 before risk, support, or margin. The owner had been quoting $1,150 and wondering why the month never added up.

### 2. Add risk and support allowance

Every offer has hours nobody bills. The follow-up call. The "quick question" text. The rain week you make up. The client who takes five days to answer. That is support and risk, and it is real cost.

How to do it: support hours are the time you spend on the account outside the deliverables. Estimate them honestly from your last three jobs, then multiply by your hourly value. Risk allowance is a percentage on top of everything for what goes wrong: rework, a callback, a part that fails, a month with two storms. I use 10 percent for work I have done fifty times and 20 percent or more for work I have done twice.

What good looks like: "Support: 2 hours per month at $45, $90. Risk: 10 percent." Written down. So the next time a buyer asks why you are higher than the Facebook bid, you answer with a line item instead of a shrug.

The mistake: skipping this because "it is just a few texts." Pull your phone log for one account. Count them.

Example: Reyes Bookkeeping, Kilgore, fictional. The 90-day cleanup. Labor: 40 hours at $65, $2,600. Software seat, $120. Support: six hours of calls and chasing receipts, $390. Risk: 15 percent, because half her clients' books are worse than they admit on the first call. That is about $3,577 before margin. Her old price was $2,500 flat. She had been doing a thousand dollars of free work per client and calling it customer service.

### 3. Choose a testable price

The floor is cost plus a margin you choose. Above it, you are picking a price to test, not a price to defend forever.

How to do it: state your margin assumption as a number. Twenty-five percent on cost is a common starting point for service work. Write it down as an assumption, because that is what it is. Then pick two prices. A founding price for your first three to five buyers, still above the floor, in exchange for something you need: permission to photograph, a written reference, a fast decision. And a regular price after that. Put a real end condition on the founding price, "first three properties" or "through October." Not a fake countdown. A limit you will honor.

Then package it good, better, best if it helps the buyer choose. Good is the scope card. Better adds the one thing buyers keep asking for. Best adds one more. Do not pretend you know the market wants three tiers. You find that out in lesson eight.

What good looks like: "Floor $1,020 per month. Founding $1,050, first three properties, includes permission to photograph and a written reference. Regular $1,250."

The mistake: skipping the floor and going straight to "what will they pay." What they will pay is a question for the validation week, and you will only hear the answer clearly if you already know your bottom.

The calculators at /tools are free. Use them to check your math, not to replace it.

Now run the prompt with your real inputs in the brackets. It shows the math and gives good-better-best packaging. Check every line against your worksheet. If the model's hours look prettier than yours, yours are right.

## Worked example

Pine Curve Lawn and Landscape, Gilmer. Fictional. Dale took his scope card, pulled his crew's hours off the last two months of timesheets for a similar property, and filled the brackets: labor hours 24, hourly value $26 loaded, hard costs $80 per month, support hours 2 at $45, risk allowance 10 percent. He pasted the exact working prompt into ChatGPT.

The first pass came back with a floor of $1,092. Reading the labor line, he saw the problem. His 24 crew hours already included the monthly walk-through, and support counted it again. He moved the walk-through to support only, changed labor to 22, and ran it again.

Example output:

```text
Labor: 22 hours x $26 = $572
Hard costs: $80
Support: 2 hours x $45 = $90
Direct cost: $742
Risk allowance 10%: $74. Cost with risk: $816
Margin assumption 25% on cost: $204. Price floor: $1,020 per property per month
Good: schedule, weekly visits, photo log, contact line, walk-through. Better: adds a quarterly bed refresh. Best: adds seasonal color and one storm cleanup per quarter. Market demand is unknown; test one tier before committing to three.
```

Review against the pass standard. Does the selected price cover direct cost, support, risk, and a stated margin assumption? Direct cost $742 with support inside it, risk $74, margin stated at 25 percent. Yes.

The one revision was catching that double count, which dropped the floor by $72. Then he chose his numbers. Founding: $1,050 per month for the first three properties, in exchange for written permission to photograph the common areas and one reference. Regular: $1,250 per month.

Then he did the thing that makes it real. He said "twelve fifty a month" out loud to his wife across the kitchen table. Twice. The second time it came out flat and normal. That is when a price is ready to test.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Help me calculate a price floor for [offer]. Use these inputs: labor hours [x], hourly value [x], hard costs [x], support hours [x], risk allowance [x percent]. Show the math and give good-better-best packaging without pretending to know market demand.
```

## Guided practice

1. Go deliverable by deliverable on your scope card and write real hours from your last three jobs, not estimates from hope.
2. Set a loaded hourly value that includes taxes and insurance, and price your own time at what a replacement would cost.
3. Count support hours from one real account's call and text log, and choose a risk percentage you can defend.
4. Paste your inputs into the exact working prompt in ChatGPT or Claude and check every line of the math against the workbook Pricing math page.
5. Write your margin assumption, then choose a founding price with a real end condition and a regular price, both above the floor.

## Pass standard

The selected price covers direct cost, support, risk, and a stated margin assumption.

## Common mistakes

- Pricing your own hours at zero. Fix: use the rate you would pay someone competent to replace you.
- Using base wages instead of loaded cost. Fix: add payroll tax and insurance before you multiply.
- Skipping support because it is "just a few texts." Fix: count them on one real account for one month.
- A founding price below the floor to land the first client. Fix: founding is a discount from regular, never below the floor.
- A fake deadline on the founding price. Fix: name the real limit, three clients or a date, and honor it.
