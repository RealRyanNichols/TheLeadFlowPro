# OE L04: Build the Proof Plan

## What you will finish

Identify ethical proof that reduces buyer uncertainty. When you are done you will have a proof inventory with a source link on every line, one unsupported claim deleted from your public materials, and the next proof asset you are going to make, named and dated.

## Why this matters

I was reading a roofer's website in Henderson. Top of the page: "#1 roofer in East Texas." I asked the owner, according to who? He laughed. A web guy typed it in 2019. Nobody had checked it since, and nobody had believed it either.

Here is what a buyer does with a claim. They read it, and they ask, quietly, "says who?" If the page cannot answer, the claim costs you trust instead of earning it. And the buyer who has been burned before, which is most of the people in your buyer brief, is scanning for the first thing that smells like a stretch.

Proof is not star ratings and "great to work with." Proof is anything a stranger can check. A count. A date. A photo with a date on it. A document. A named client who agreed to be named. That is why this lesson comes before price. What you can prove changes what you can ask, and how much risk you have to carry.

Bring the scope card from lesson three. Every claim you make in public should trace back to something on that card or to a fact you can point at.

## The lesson

### 1. Inventory existing evidence

Most owners have more proof than they think and less than they claim. Start by collecting what already exists.

How to do it: open the workbook Proof inventory page or a spreadsheet. Four columns: claim, evidence, source link or file, label. Now walk your phone, your invoices, your Google Business Profile, your job photo folder, your certificates, your insurance. Every fact about your business goes on a line.

What good looks like: "In business since 2019. Evidence: state filing and first invoice. Link." "Fourteen properties under contract. Evidence: signed agreements in Drive. Link." "General liability insurance. Evidence: current certificate. File." "Photo of a common area, June 12. Evidence: dated photo, board permission by email. Link."

The mistake: only counting testimonials. A count of jobs with dates is stronger than a compliment. And permission matters. Ask before you name a client or show their property. Get it in writing, even a text, and save the text as the source.

Example: my own site, RealRyanNichols.com, has a records archive with more than 1,500 entries. I can say that because the number is on the site and you can count. I cannot say "one of the largest archives in Texas," because I never measured the others. Same fact, two claims, one of them provable.

### 2. Separate facts from claims

Now label every line. Verified: you can show it right now. Supported but incomplete: true as far as you know, but the evidence is partial or the wording is bigger than the evidence. Unsupported: you cannot show it.

How to do it: read each claim the way a skeptical buyer would. Say "prove it" out loud. If the proof is a link or a file, verified. If the proof is "I have been doing this a long time," incomplete. If the proof is a feeling, unsupported.

What good looks like: most public claims land in verified, a few in incomplete with a fix written next to them, and every unsupported claim marked for deletion. Best, top, leading, painless, proven, fastest, and any number about a client's results you did not measure go straight to unsupported unless you are holding a document.

The mistake: rewriting an unsupported claim to sound softer instead of removing it. "One of the best" is not more honest than "the best." It is the same claim wearing a hedge.

Example: Sabine Strength, Longview, fictional. "Members lose 20 pounds in six weeks." Unsupported, no records, and a health claim a gym should not be making anyway. Removed. Replaced with the scope card: "Six coached sessions, a written program, a form check on three lifts." Whitfield Family Dental in Marshall, fictional, had "painless dentistry" on the door. Removed. Replaced with "same-week appointments for new patients," which the front desk can show on the calendar.

### 3. Plan the next proof asset

For every gap, choose the smallest honest asset that closes it. Small is the point. You are not producing a case study video this week. You are getting one dated photo, one permission email, one sample document with the name blurred.

How to do it: look at the supported-but-incomplete lines first, because they are the cheapest to fix. Then pick the one gap where the buyer's uncertainty is highest. Usually it is "will this actually get done" or "what does the finished thing look like." Make the asset that answers that. Put a due date on the calendar.

What good looks like: "Sample monthly walk-through report from a current property, name blurred, board permission by email. Due Friday." "Before and after of one reconciled month, account numbers blurred, client permission by text. Due Tuesday." Specific, permissioned, small.

The mistake: reaching for a testimonial you have to ask for and then wait on. Make the thing you can make alone first. A finished sample of your own work is proof that you finish.

If a tool is part of your offer, the tool itself is proof. That is why the calculators at /tools are free on my site. A buyer can use one before we ever talk, and it tells them more than a paragraph about me would.

Example: Big Pine Roofing, Henderson, fictional. Gap: homeowners cannot tell whether the crew cleans up. Smallest asset: a twenty-second phone video of the magnet sweep for nails at the end of a job, dated, with the homeowner's permission by text. Four minutes of work. It answers the exact question the buyer was not going to ask out loud.

Now run the prompt. Paste your offer, meaning the buyer brief, the result statement, and the scope card, plus every claim from your inventory with its evidence. It labels each claim and recommends the smallest ethical proof asset for each gap. Check its labels against your own. If it calls something verified and you cannot find the file, it is not verified.

## Worked example

Pine Curve Lawn and Landscape, Gilmer. Fictional. Dale pulled every claim off his website, his truck doors, and his Facebook page, listed his evidence next to each one, and pasted the offer and the list into the exact working prompt.

Example output:

```text
"Serving Gilmer since 2019." Verified. Source: state filing, first invoice. Keep.
"14 properties under contract." Verified. Source: signed agreements. Keep, update quarterly.
"Fully insured." Verified. Source: current certificate. Offer to show it on request.
"We have never missed a scheduled visit." Supported but incomplete. Visit logs begin January 2025. Rewrite: "No missed scheduled visits since we began logging in January 2025."
"Gilmer's most reliable lawn service." Unsupported. Remove.
"Boards love working with us." Unsupported. Remove, or replace with one named quote with written permission.
Smallest next asset: one sample monthly walk-through report from a current property, name blurred, board permission in writing.
```

Review against the pass standard. Can every public claim be traced to evidence, or is it clearly labeled as an example, estimate, or opinion? After the two removals, yes. The "never missed" line was the important catch. It was true in Dale's head and unprovable before 2025.

The one revision: Dale did not just delete "most reliable." He replaced that spot on the page with the logged fact and a date: "No missed scheduled visits since January 2025, with a photo log for every one." Same idea, now checkable by anyone who asks.

Then he texted the president of the board he has served longest and asked permission to share a blurred copy of last month's report. She said yes in eleven minutes. He saved the text in the inventory as the source and put "Friday" next to the asset. That is the difference between a proof plan and a wish list. Every line has a link, and the next asset has a date.

## Guided practice

1. Copy every claim off your website, truck, signs, and social pages into the workbook Proof inventory.
2. Next to each one, write the evidence and paste a link or file name; leave it blank if there is nothing.
3. Paste your offer and the list into the exact working prompt in ChatGPT or Claude and compare its labels to yours.
4. Delete one unsupported claim from a live page today and put a dated fact in its place.
5. Name the next proof asset, get the permission you need in writing, and put a due date on it.

## Assignment and evidence

Create a proof inventory with source links and remove one unsupported claim.

Save the source material, the prompt, the first result, the final result, and your review notes.

## Pass standard

Every public claim can be traced to evidence or is clearly labeled as an example, estimate, or opinion.

## Common mistakes

- Treating star ratings as the only proof. Fix: counts, dates, documents, and dated photos come first.
- Softening a claim instead of removing it. Fix: if you cannot show it, delete it and put a fact in its place.
- Naming clients or showing their property without asking. Fix: permission in writing, even a text, saved as the source.
- Planning a big case study instead of a small sample. Fix: make the thing you can finish alone this week.
- Labeling from memory. Fix: verified means you clicked the link while you labeled it.
