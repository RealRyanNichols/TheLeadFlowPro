# OE L02: Define the Result

## What you will finish

State a useful outcome without promising revenue, rankings, virality, or certainty. When you are done you will have one result statement and one limitations statement on the workbook Result page, both plain enough to read to a buyer over the phone without clearing your throat.

## Why this matters

A coach in Longview, fictional, promised a client "double your revenue in 90 days." The client worked hard. Revenue went up about fifteen percent. He asked for a refund, because that is what he was promised. The coach did good work and still lost, because the promise was the wrong thing to promise.

The opposite failure is just as common. A roofer's page says "quality you can trust." The homeowner reads it and still does not know what shows up in the driveway, when, or what happens when it rains. Vague results turn into scope fights later, because nobody agreed on what done means.

This lesson sits between the buyer and the scope on purpose. Bring the buyer brief from lesson one. You are going to write what will exist for that buyer when you are finished, in words that are true on your worst day, not your best one.

Next lesson turns that result into a scope card. You cannot package a fog.

## The lesson

Here is the rule I use. Promise the thing you control. Describe the thing you hope for. Never mix the two in one sentence.

### 1. Separate output from outcome

An output is what you make. A mowed common area, a reconciled set of books, a new roof, a live website. An outcome is what the buyer hopes happens next. Fewer complaints, a calm April, more calls, a dry living room.

You control the output. You influence the outcome. The buyer buys because of the outcome, so you have to talk about it. But you only promise the output.

How to do it: draw two columns on the Result page. Left, what will exist when you are done. Right, what the buyer hopes changes because of it. Write the offer from the left column. Speak about the right column with the words "so that" and "built to."

What good looks like: "You will have a 12-month maintenance schedule, a completed first visit with dated photos, and one number to text, so that you stop chasing your vendor." The first half is promised. The second half is why it matters.

The mistake: putting the outcome in the promise. "We will stop the complaints." You cannot. Residents complain about things you do not control, including the neighbor's dog.

Example: Keisha Bell, a realtor in Tyler, fictional. Output: professional photos, a staging checklist, a pricing comparison from the last six months of nearby sales, and the listing live within seven days. Outcome she hopes for: sold in thirty days. She promises the first list. She works toward the second and says so.

### 2. Bound the time and scope

A result without an edge is a trap. "Ongoing support" means forever. "We handle your books" means everything. Put a fence around it.

How to do it: answer three questions inside the result statement. When does it start and when does it end? What is inside the fence? What is outside it? Use a number for time. Thirty days, six weeks, one quarter.

What good looks like: "Within 30 days of signing" beats "quickly." "Mowing, edging, trimming, and bed weeding" beats "lawn care." "Does not include tree removal or irrigation repair" beats silence.

The mistake: leaving the edges to be discussed later. Later is when the board president asks about the ditch by the entrance and you have already quoted the price.

Example: Reyes Bookkeeping, Kilgore, fictional. Marisol's first draft: "I get your books in order." Bounded: "In 90 days you will have twelve months of reconciled books, a chart of accounts that matches how you bid jobs, and a one-page monthly close checklist your office manager can run. Catch-up beyond twelve months is quoted separately." A plumber can picture that. He cannot picture "in order."

### 3. Remove unsupported promises

Now hunt the draft for words you cannot back. Revenue. Rankings. Leads. Best. Painless. In no time. Any number about the buyer's business that you did not measure.

How to do it: read every sentence and ask, "If this does not happen, does the buyer get to say I lied?" If yes, cut the word or move it to the "so that" side with a softer verb.

Then write the limitations statement. Two to four sentences, plain language, that say what you do not control. This is not a legal disclaimer. It is the honest paragraph that makes the rest of the page believable. If your work touches contracts or consent, have your attorney review the wording once and move on.

What good looks like: "I control the schedule, the crew, and the photos. I do not control the weather, your residents, or the board vote. Rain days are made up within three business days."

The mistake: believing limits scare buyers off. The buyer who has been burned before, which is most of your buyer brief, is looking for the person who says what they will not do. The hype is what scares them.

Example: Sabine Strength, a gym in Longview, fictional. The first draft of a six-week intro program said "lose 15 pounds." Cut. Final: "Six coached sessions, a written program you keep, and a form check on the three main lifts. What you do with it after week six is up to you." That is a promise a gym can keep on a bad week.

Now run the prompt. Paste your broadest service line into the bracket, the one off your website or your truck. It returns five bounded statements. Pick the one that fits your buyer brief, then rewrite it in your own words. The version you would say out loud is the version that goes on the page.

## Worked example

Pine Curve Lawn and Landscape, Gilmer. Fictional. Bring the buyer brief from lesson one: HOA board presidents and part-time property managers with a mow vendor who keeps skipping weeks.

Dale's broad service line, straight off his website: "Full-service lawn care and landscaping for residential and commercial properties."

He pasted the exact working prompt into ChatGPT with the bracket replaced by that sentence. Five statements came back. This is the one that matched the brief.

Example output:

```text
For: HOA boards and property managers with common areas of up to three acres.
Will finish: Within 30 days of signing, a written 12-month maintenance schedule, your first two weekly service visits completed with dated photos sent to one contact, and one phone line with next-business-day response.
Included: Mowing, edging, string trimming, bed weeding, and blowing on every scheduled visit.
Not promised: Fewer resident complaints, board renewal, or any change in property values. Weather delays are rescheduled, not skipped.
You will finally have peace of mind about your common areas.
```

Review against the pass standard. Can a buyer tell what will exist at the end? Yes: a schedule, two completed visits with photos, a phone line. Does the language avoid promising business results? Almost. The last line promises peace of mind, and Dale cannot deliver a feeling.

The one revision: he cut the last line and wrote the limitations statement under the result instead. "I control the schedule, the crew, and the photos. I do not control the weather, your residents, or the board vote. Rain days are made up within three business days."

Then he read both statements to a board president he already mows for. She said, "So if it rains Tuesday you come Thursday?" That question told him the statement worked. She was already picturing it.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Rewrite this broad service into five bounded outcome statements: [service]. Each must say who it is for, what they will finish, what is included, and what is not guaranteed.
```

## Guided practice

1. Copy your broadest service sentence from your website or Facebook page onto the workbook Result page.
2. Draw two columns, output and outcome, and sort every claim you make into one of them.
3. Paste the exact working prompt into ChatGPT or Claude with your service in the bracket, and pick the one statement that fits your buyer brief.
4. Rewrite it in your own words with a time limit, what is included, and what is not.
5. Write the limitations statement, then read both aloud to one person who fits the buyer brief.

## Assignment and evidence

Write one final result statement and a plain-language limitations statement.

Save the source material, the prompt, the first result, the final result, and your review notes.

## Pass standard

A buyer can tell what will exist at the end, while the language avoids guaranteed business results.

## Common mistakes

- Promising the outcome instead of the output. Fix: if you cannot make it happen with your own hands, it goes after "so that."
- No time boundary. Fix: put a number of days or weeks in the first sentence.
- Softening the limits into legal mush. Fix: write them the way you would say them across a truck hood.
- Keeping "best," "painless," or "in no time" because it sounds good. Fix: cut it, or replace it with something you can show.
- Letting the five versions replace your own voice. Fix: pick one, then rewrite it out loud.
