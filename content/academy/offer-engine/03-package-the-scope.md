# OE L03: Package the Scope

## What you will finish

Create a scope that is easy to understand, deliver, and review. When you are done you will have a scope card with no more than five deliverables, each with an owner, a format, a review point, and a boundary, ready to hand to a buyer or to a crew lead on a Tuesday.

## Why this matters

Here is how a good contract goes bad. Dale agreed to mow the common areas. Then the board asked about the flowerbeds. Then the ditch by the entrance. Then the lights on the neighborhood sign. Nothing was written down, so every "one more thing" was free. By month four the contract that looked good on paper was losing money, and he was quietly resenting a client who thought everything was fine.

That is what a missing scope costs. Not one big loss. A hundred small ones nobody can point to.

A scope is also how you review your own work. If nobody can say what done looks like, nobody can say whether you did it, including you. And the scope card is what makes the next two lessons possible. You cannot put a price floor on work with no edges, and you cannot prove a result you never defined.

Bring the result statement from lesson two. We are going to turn it into something a new hire could deliver without calling you.

## The lesson

### 1. List required inputs

Before you promise a deliverable, list what you need from the buyer to produce it. Access, files, decisions, and a person who answers questions.

How to do it: walk through the job in your head from day one. Every time you stop and think "I would need to ask them for that," write it down. Gate codes. The last twelve months of bank statements. The login for the domain. A decision on the shingle color before you order material.

What good looks like: a short list with a due date on each item. "Property map and gate codes before the first visit." "Read-only bank access by day three." "One person who can answer questions within two business days."

The mistake: assuming you will get it as you go. As you go is how a 90-day job becomes a 200-day job, and the buyer blames you for the delay they caused.

Example: Reyes Bookkeeping, Kilgore, fictional. Inputs: QuickBooks admin invite, read-only bank and card access, twelve months of statements, a list of trucks and crews, and one person who can answer "what was this $840 at the supply house" within two business days. Next to the last one she wrote: "If nobody answers, the 90 days pause." That one line has saved her more grief than any contract clause.

### 2. Define deliverables

A deliverable is a thing that exists when you are done. It has an owner, the person who produces it. A format: PDF, photo set, spreadsheet, a live page, a physical result. A review point, when the buyer looks at it and says yes. And a boundary, what it does not include.

How to do it: cap it at five. If you have nine, some of them are steps, not deliverables. Steps go in the process. Deliverables go on the card. Give each one all four fields, and write the boundary as a sentence that starts with "does not include."

What good looks like: "Written 12-month maintenance schedule. Owner: Dale. Format: one-page PDF. Review point: manager approves before the first visit. Does not include seasonal color or irrigation."

The mistake: deliverables that are verbs. "Ongoing communication" is not a deliverable. "A photo log texted after each visit, four to six dated photos" is.

Example: Caddo Plumbing, Longview, fictional. Water heater replacement. Deliverables: the installed unit with model and serial on the invoice, the old unit hauled off, the permit number where the city requires one, a photo of the finished install, and a one-page care sheet. Five. The owner used to say "we replace water heaters." Now a tech on his second day knows exactly what done means.

### 3. Set revision and approval limits

Revisions are where margin goes to die. Approvals are where projects go to wait. Put a number on both.

How to do it: for anything the buyer reviews, say how many rounds are included and what a round is. Say how long they have to respond and what happens if they do not. Then write the acceptance criteria, the plain test that says a deliverable is accepted.

What good looks like: "One round of category corrections after the day-30 review. Corrections after that are billed hourly." "Schedule approved within five business days, or the first visit slides a week." "Accepted when the manager signs the monthly walk-through report."

The mistake: no limit because you want to be easy to work with. Easy to work with is showing up on time. Unlimited revisions are not kindness. They are an unpriced risk, and buyers respect a clear line more than a vague yes.

Write the exclusions in the same plain voice as the deliverables. "Does not include tree removal, irrigation repair, or storm debris beyond one trailer load. Those are quoted separately in writing." Do not bury them at the bottom. The buyer who reads the exclusions and signs anyway is the buyer you want.

Example: Keisha Bell, the Tyler realtor, fictional. Her listing prep scope now says: "One staging walk-through and one revision to the photo set if the seller changes the furniture. Additional shoots billed at cost." She said it felt cold. Her next seller read it and said, "Good, so I know what I am getting."

Now run the prompt with your result statement pasted in. It returns inputs, deliverables, milestones, client responsibilities, exclusions, revision policy, and acceptance criteria. Move the deliverables onto the scope card, cap them at five, and fill in the four fields on each one by hand.

## Worked example

Pine Curve Lawn and Landscape, Gilmer. Fictional. Bring the result statement from lesson two: within 30 days, a 12-month schedule, the first two visits with dated photos, and one phone line with next-business-day response.

Dale pasted that statement into the exact working prompt in ChatGPT.

Example output:

```text
Deliverable 1: 12-month maintenance schedule. Owner: Pine Curve. Format: one-page PDF. Review: manager approves before visit one. Does not include seasonal color or irrigation.
Deliverable 2: Weekly service visit with checklist. Owner: crew lead. Format: signed checklist per visit. Review: manager may flag within 48 hours.
Deliverable 3: Photo log after each visit. Owner: crew lead. Format: four to six dated photos by text. Review: monthly.
Deliverable 4: Monthly 20-minute walk-through. Owner: Dale. Format: in person with a one-page report. Review: manager signs the report.
Deliverable 5: Single contact line. Owner: Dale. Format: business number with next-business-day response. Review: monthly.
Exclusions: tree removal, irrigation repair, storm debris beyond one trailer load, holiday lighting.
Revision policy: schedule revised once per quarter at no charge; add-ons quoted in writing.
```

Review against the pass standard. Does every deliverable have an owner, a format, a review point, and a clear boundary? The first four did. The fifth, the contact line, had no boundary at all. What counts as an emergency? What about Sunday night?

The one revision: Dale wrote the boundary for deliverable five. "Text or call between 7 am and 6 pm, Monday through Saturday. Same-day response during those hours, next business day otherwise. Storm damage is scheduled, not promised same day." He put that number on a Quo business line so it rings his phone and logs every message, instead of a personal cell that vanishes when he changes trucks.

Then he handed the card to his crew lead and asked one question: "Could you deliver this without me?" The crew lead read it twice and said yes. That is the whole test.

## Pass standard

Every deliverable has an owner, format, review point, and clear boundary.

## Common mistakes

- Nine deliverables. Fix: if it is a step toward a thing, it is not the thing; move it to the process.
- Deliverables that are verbs. Fix: rewrite each one as a noun you could photograph or open.
- No inputs list. Fix: walk the job in your head and write down every "I would need to ask them for that."
- Unlimited revisions to seem easygoing. Fix: one round included, then hourly, in writing.
- Exclusions hidden at the bottom in small type. Fix: same voice, same size, right under the deliverables.
