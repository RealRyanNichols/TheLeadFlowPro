# OE L06: Answer Real Objections

## What you will finish

Respond to hesitation with clarity, proof, fit, and boundaries. When you are done you will have an objection bank of eight answers in your own words, tested on one real person, ready to become the FAQ section of your offer page.

## Why this matters

The buyer says "let me think about it," and you never hear from them again. You tell yourself they were not serious. Most of the time they had one question they did not feel comfortable asking, and you never gave them a place to ask it.

There are two bad ways to handle that. The first is to say nothing and hope. The second is pressure. A countdown. "Price goes up Friday." A third follow-up text that reads like a collections call. Pressure works on a few people once. Then they refund, or they tell three friends, and in a town of forty thousand that is a lot of friends.

The honest way is to know the eight things people actually hesitate about and answer them before they have to ask. Not with a script. With the truth, including the limits. Bring the proof inventory from lesson four and the price-floor worksheet from lesson five. The two biggest objections are "can I trust you" and "is it worth it," and you now have real answers to both.

This comes before the offer page because the FAQ is built from this bank, not from what a marketer imagines people ask.

## The lesson

### 1. Collect exact language

Do not write objections from memory. Memory cleans them up. Collect them the way people actually said them.

How to do it: open your text messages, your email, your Quo call log or whatever you use, your Facebook messages, and your quote follow-ups. Look for the moment someone slowed down. Copy the exact words. "Y'all are a little higher than the other guy." "I need to run it by my husband." "We got burned last time." "What if it rains?" Put them on the workbook Objection bank page, one per line, word for word, with the date and where it came from.

If you do not have eight yet, ask. Call three past buyers who said yes and ask what almost made them say no. Call two who said no and ask what the deciding thing was. People will tell you when you are not selling.

What good looks like: eight to twelve lines in the buyer's voice, not yours.

The mistake: writing objections in business language. "Concern about pricing relative to competitors" is not what anyone said. "Y'all are higher" is.

Example: Whitfield Family Dental, Marshall, fictional. The front desk kept hearing "I don't have insurance" and ending the conversation there. Once they wrote it down word for word, they realized it was never about insurance. It was "what will this cost me, and will you tell me before you start."

### 2. Identify the uncertainty

Every objection is a question underneath. Your job is to find the question, because the words on top are usually not the real thing.

How to do it: next to each line, write "what are they actually unsure about?" It is almost always one of five. Will this work for someone like me? Can I trust you to show up? What happens if it goes wrong? Is the price fair for what I get? Do I have to decide right now? Match every line to one.

What good looks like: "We got burned last time" becomes "can I trust you to show up, and what happens when you do not." "Our current guy is cheaper" becomes "is the extra money buying anything I can see." "I need to run it by the board" becomes "I cannot decide alone and I do not want to be pushed."

The mistake: answering the surface words. When someone says "too expensive" and you drop the price, you just told them the first price was made up, and you still did not answer the question they had.

Example: Reyes Bookkeeping, Kilgore, fictional. "I'm not comfortable giving you bank access." Underneath: "what can you do with that access, and who else can see it." The answer was read-only access with no ability to move money, one named person, and a one-page document that says so. Price never came up again.

### 3. Answer without pressure

Now write each answer in plain language, the way you would say it standing in the buyer's driveway. Four parts. Name the real uncertainty. Answer it directly, with a fact from your proof inventory where you have one. State the limit, the thing you do not control or do not include. Give one low-pressure next step.

How to do it: keep each answer under a hundred words. If you have a fact, use the fact. If you do not, say so. Never invent a deadline. If the founding price really ends after three clients, say that, and say it the same way to everyone.

What good looks like: "We got burned last time. I hear that from boards a lot, and it is usually about no-shows. Every visit gets a signed checklist and four to six dated photos texted to you the same day. Since we started logging in January 2025 we have not missed a scheduled visit. If one is ever missed, it is made up within three business days, in writing. I do not control the weather, but I do control whether you hear from me. If it helps, I can send you last month's report from a current property, name blurred."

The mistake: ending with a push. "So let's get you signed up today!" Cut it. The next step should be something the buyer can say yes to without paying: see a sample, walk the property, talk to a current client, take the week.

Then test it. Read all eight to one real person who fits the buyer brief. Not a friend. Watch their face. Where they frown, the answer is not clear yet. Where they ask a follow-up question, you just found objection nine.

Example: Sabine Strength, Longview, fictional. "I've tried gyms before and quit." Real uncertainty: "will I be embarrassed and waste money again." Answer: six sessions with a coach, a written program you keep, and week one is a form check, not a workout you cannot finish. Limit: it only works if you show up for all six. Next step: come watch a session before you pay anything. The owner read it to a member's sister. She said, "Okay, that I could do." That is a pass.

Now run the prompt with your collected lines pasted in. It returns the real uncertainty, a plain answer, the limitation, and the next step for each. Rewrite every answer in your own words before it goes anywhere near your page.

## Worked example

Pine Curve Lawn and Landscape, Gilmer. Fictional. Dale went through six months of texts and quote follow-up emails and pulled eight lines, word for word: "Our current guy is cheaper." "We got burned by the last company." "What happens when it rains all week?" "Are you insured?" "We don't want to be locked into twelve months." "Who do we actually call when something's wrong?" "Can you also do the trees and the sprinklers?" "The board has to vote, I can't decide today."

He pasted all eight into the exact working prompt in ChatGPT.

Example output:

```text
Objection: "We don't want to be locked into twelve months."
Real uncertainty: What if you stop showing up and we are stuck paying for it.
Answer: The schedule runs twelve months so the property is planned for every season, but the agreement can be ended with 30 days written notice after the first 90 days.
Limitation: The founding price applies only to twelve-month schedules. Month-to-month is regular price.
Next step: Read the one-page agreement first. No signature needed to see it.
```

Review against the pass standard. Does the answer address the concern directly? Yes. The fear is being stuck, and the 30-day exit answers it. Does it include limits? Yes, the founding price condition. Does it manufacture urgency? No.

The one revision: the model's answer to "the board has to vote" ended with "I would be happy to attend your next meeting to close the deal." Dale cut "close the deal." His version: "Boards vote. That is how it should work. I can send a one-page summary you can forward, and if it helps I will come to the meeting and answer questions for fifteen minutes. Take the time you need."

Then he read all eight to the manager of the office park on Highway 271, the third name from his buyer brief. She frowned at the rain answer. "Three business days is a long time in May." He changed it to "made up within three business days, and during growing season you move to the front of the next dry day." Objection answered, and he had not spent a dollar on ads yet.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Turn these buyer objections into honest FAQ answers: [paste objections]. For each, identify the real uncertainty, answer in plain language, state any limitation, and give a low-pressure next step.
```

## Guided practice

1. Pull eight objections word for word from your texts, emails, call log, and quote follow-ups, and paste them onto the workbook Objection bank page.
2. Next to each one, write the real uncertainty in one line.
3. Paste the eight into the exact working prompt in ChatGPT or Claude and read what comes back.
4. Rewrite every answer in your own voice with a fact from your proof inventory, a limit, and one no-commitment next step.
5. Read all eight to one real person who fits your buyer brief and change anything that made them frown.

## Assignment and evidence

Write eight objection answers and test them with one real person.

Save the source material, the prompt, the first result, the final result, and your review notes.

## Pass standard

Answers address the concern directly, include limits, and do not manufacture urgency.

## Common mistakes

- Writing objections from memory in your own words. Fix: copy the exact text from a real message.
- Dropping the price when someone says "too much." Fix: answer what the money buys, with a line item from the worksheet.
- Ending every answer with a push. Fix: the next step is something they can say yes to without paying.
- Answering the surface words instead of the uncertainty. Fix: write "what are they actually unsure about" first.
- Testing on a friend. Fix: test on someone who fits the brief and has no reason to be nice to you.
