# OE L01: Choose One Buyer

## What you will finish

Name a specific buyer whose problem you understand well enough to solve. When you are done you will have a one-page buyer brief on the workbook Buyer page, and three real names written under it, people or businesses you could text this week.

## Why this matters

Most owners I meet in East Texas have a website that says "we do it all." Mowing, landscaping, irrigation, fence repair, Christmas lights. Bookkeeping, payroll, taxes, "and more." It feels safe. It is not.

Here is the scene. The phone rings while you are under a house or on a roof. It is someone asking for the one thing you barely do. You quote it anyway because money is money. You win it at a bad price, it eats a week, and the good call, the one you were built for, went to voicemail and called the next guy.

An offer for everyone gets priced for no one. You cannot define a result, write a scope, or set a price until you know who is on the other end. That is why this lesson comes first. Every page after this one starts with the buyer you name today.

This is not about turning work away forever. It is about deciding who your best offer is built for and saying so out loud. Take the odd job if you want. Just stop building the business around it.

## The lesson

If you took The ChatGPT Operator, you know how to run a prompt. If you did not, here is everything you need for this course: open ChatGPT or Claude, paste the exact working prompt, replace the bracket with your own words, and answer its questions with facts. That is it.

### 1. Start with lived evidence

Open the workbook Buyer page or a blank Google Doc. Write down your last ten paid jobs. Real ones. Name, town, what they bought, what they paid, how long it took, and one line about how it went.

Now circle the three you would take again tomorrow. Not the biggest. The ones that paid on time, did not fight the invoice, and told somebody about you.

What good looks like: you can see a pattern in the circles. Same kind of person, same kind of problem, same reason they called. Maybe it is the property manager with a vendor who keeps skipping weeks. Maybe it is the contractor whose books are six months behind.

The mistake: starting with who you wish would buy. "High-end clients." "Bigger companies." Wishes are not evidence. Your bank deposits are evidence. Your call log is evidence. The jobs you dread are evidence too, because they tell you who the offer is not for.

Example: Big Pine Roofing in Henderson is a fictional example. The owner lists forty jobs and circles three. All homeowners with a roof over fifteen years old. All called after the same hail event. All had an adjuster already scheduled. That is a buyer. "Anyone who needs a roof" was never a buyer.

### 2. Name the costly problem

A buyer is a person with a problem that costs them something they can feel. Money, time, sleep, embarrassment in front of a board. Write the problem in the buyer's words, not yours.

Here is the test. Say the sentence to that person's face. If they would nod, you have it. If they would say "well, kind of," keep digging.

What good looks like: one sentence, no jargon. "The mow crew skips weeks and the residents complain to me." "The CPA sends me forty questions every April because my books are a mess." "My phone rings while I am under a house and I never call them back."

Put a number next to it when you honestly can. Not a promise. A cost. If you miss calls, run your own numbers through /tools/missed-call-calculator. It shows what a missed call is worth to you, and the same math works for your buyer.

The mistake: naming a problem the buyer does not feel yet. "Their brand is inconsistent." Nobody in Kilgore lies awake over brand consistency. They lie awake over the payroll deposit on Friday.

Example: Reyes Bookkeeping in Kilgore, fictional. Marisol started with "small businesses that need bookkeeping." After listing her last ten clients, she saw that the best three were all plumbing and HVAC owners doing their own books at the kitchen table. The costly problem, in their words: "I cannot tell which jobs made money, and every April is a fire drill." That sentence is the whole offer, pointed the right way.

### 3. Exclude poor-fit buyers

This is the part owners skip because it feels rude. It is not rude. It is honest, and it saves both of you a bad month.

Write a short "not for" list. Who calls you that you should send somewhere else? Who costs you money every time? Who wants a result you cannot deliver?

What good looks like: three to five lines a stranger could apply without asking you. "Not for one-time cleanups." "Not for businesses more than three years behind on their books." "Not for anyone choosing on the lowest bid."

The mistake: making the list so soft it excludes nobody. "Not for people who are not serious" is not a line. It is a mood.

Example: Pine Curve Lawn and Landscape in Gilmer, fictional, and you will see them in every lesson of this course. Dale wrote: not for homeowners wanting a one-time cleanup, not for apartment complexes that need a daily crew, not for boards that pick the cheapest bid every year. He said it felt like turning down money. Then he looked at his ten-job list and saw those were exactly the jobs that lost money.

Now run the exact working prompt. It asks five questions one at a time. Answer from your ten-job list, not from memory, and if it asks something you do not know, say "I do not know" instead of guessing. When it returns the brief, read it against the pass standard: specific, observable, reachable, and connected to a problem you can honestly help solve. If the buyer description could fit half the county, answer the questions again with more detail.

## Worked example

Pine Curve Lawn and Landscape, Gilmer. Fictional. Two crews, one trailer, and a website that lists eleven services.

Input: Dale's last ten paid jobs. Six residential mows, one sod install, one set of Christmas lights, and three HOA common-area contracts. The three HOAs paid on time, renewed, and one of them referred him. Two of the residential jobs ended in arguments over the invoice.

He opened ChatGPT and pasted the exact working prompt with the bracket replaced by "common-area lawn maintenance for HOAs and small office parks." It asked five questions, one at a time: who paid you most recently and why, what did they try before you, what happened right before they called, what did they complain about, and who have you turned away.

Example output:

```text
Buyer description: Volunteer HOA board presidents and part-time property managers responsible for common areas in 20 to 80 home neighborhoods within 25 miles of Gilmer.
Urgent problem: The current crew skips weeks, residents complain at meetings, and the president has to chase the vendor around a day job.
Failed alternatives: Lowest bid from a Facebook post, a regional company that treats them as a small account, a resident's nephew.
Buying trigger: A missed week before a neighborhood event, or the second no-show from the current vendor.
Not for: Homeowners wanting a one-time cleanup, complexes needing daily crews, boards that choose on price alone.
```

Review against the pass standard. Specific: yes, a board president with a day job. Observable: yes, you can drive the neighborhood and see the common area. Reachable: yes, boards post meeting notices and management companies list contacts. Connected to a problem Dale can honestly solve: yes, he can show up on schedule and prove it with photos.

The one revision: the first draft said "within 60 miles of Gilmer." Dale's crews lose an hour each way past 25. He cut the radius, and the buyer got more real, not less. Then he wrote three names under the brief: the two boards he already mows for, and the office park on Highway 271 whose grass he drives past every morning and has never once called.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Act as an offer strategist. Help me define one specific buyer for [skill/service]. Ask five questions one at a time, then return: buyer description, urgent problem, failed alternatives, buying trigger, and who this is not for. Do not invent facts.
```

## Assignment and evidence

Complete a one-page ideal buyer brief and name three people or businesses that fit it.

Save the source material, the prompt, the first result, the final result, and your review notes.

## Pass standard

The buyer is specific, observable, reachable, and connected to a problem you can honestly help solve.

## Common mistakes

- Picking the buyer you wish you had instead of the one who already paid. Fix: start from the ten-job list every time.
- Describing a category instead of a person. Fix: "small businesses" is a category; "the owner doing books at the kitchen table at 10 pm" is a person.
- Letting ChatGPT fill in facts you did not give it. Fix: if a line in the brief is not something you saw or heard, delete it.
- A "not for" list that excludes nobody. Fix: name the last three jobs that lost money and write the line that would have turned them away.
- Stopping before the three names. Fix: the brief is not done until you can text somebody who fits it.
