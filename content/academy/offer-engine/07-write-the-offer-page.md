# OE L07: Write the Offer Page

## What you will finish

Assemble the buyer, problem, result, scope, proof, price, and next action into one page. When you are done you will have a full offer page draft in a document you own, read aloud once, with one call to action, ready to publish on a page you control.

## Why this matters

I have sat with owners whose websites have fourteen pages and no offer. Services, about, gallery, blog, contact. A buyer lands, reads for forty seconds, and cannot tell what to buy, what it costs, or what to do next. So they leave and call the company whose page says one thing.

Every dollar you spend on attention later, ads, yard signs, posts, referrals, lands on this page. If the page is vague, the money is wasted before it arrives. That is why I build the page before I ever run an ad, and why this lesson comes before the validation week. You need one place to send five people.

The good news: you already wrote it. The buyer brief from lesson one. The result statement from two. The scope card from three. The proof inventory from four. The prices from five. The objection bank from six. This lesson is assembly. The only new skills are order, and the discipline to have one call to action.

## The lesson

Before you start, put all six artifacts into one document. That is your approved brief. Read it top to bottom once. If anything contradicts anything else, fix it in the brief, not on the page.

### 1. Lead with the buyer's problem

The top of the page is not about you. It is about the person in the buyer brief and the problem in their words.

How to do it: headline first. One sentence a board president would recognize as her own situation. Then two or three short paragraphs that describe the problem the way it was said in the objection bank and the buyer interviews. Then the result statement from lesson two: output promised, outcome hoped for, in that order.

What good looks like: "Common areas that get done on schedule, with photos to prove it." Under it: "Your mow crew skipped a week again. Residents complained at the meeting. And you have a day job." The buyer nods. Now they keep reading.

The mistake: a headline about you. "Gilmer's trusted lawn care partner." Nobody recognizes their own life in that. The other mistake is a clever headline. Clear beats clever every time on a page a plumber reads on his phone in a parking lot.

Example: Reyes Bookkeeping, Kilgore, fictional. Old headline: "Bookkeeping, payroll, and more for small businesses." New: "Know which jobs made money before the CPA asks." Same business. Now the plumbing owner reading it feels seen.

### 2. Show the deliverable and process

This is the middle of the page. Deliverables, process, proof, fit and non-fit, price, FAQ, limitations. In that order.

How to do it: paste the five deliverables from the scope card, each as one line with its format. Then the process as three to five numbered steps from first contact to done, with time on each. Then proof, facts only, from the inventory, with dates. Then "this is for" and "this is not for" from the buyer brief. Then the price, with the founding condition stated plainly and the regular price next to it. Then the eight answers from the objection bank as the FAQ. Then the limitations statement, in the same voice as everything else.

What good looks like: a buyer can answer every question they had without calling. What do I get. How does it go. Why should I believe you. Is this for me. What does it cost. What about my worry. What do you not do.

The mistake: hiding the price. If you make people call for the price, the ones who would have paid it assume it is more than it is, and the ones who cannot afford it call anyway and cost you an hour. Put the number on the page. If the price truly varies, put the starting price and the one thing that changes it.

Example: Big Pine Roofing, Henderson, fictional. Process: 1. Free 30-minute roof inspection with photos, same week. 2. Written estimate within two business days. 3. Install scheduled within ten business days of approval. 4. Magnet sweep and photo walk-through at the end. Four steps, each with time. A homeowner can see the whole thing before she calls.

### 3. Make the next action obvious

One call to action. One. Not "call, text, email, or fill out the form." One button, one thing, repeated at the top and the bottom.

How to do it: choose the next step that matches how your buyer decides. Boards and property managers want a walk-through. Homeowners want an inspection. A contractor with messy books wants a fifteen-minute call. Name it, say how long it takes, and say what happens after. Make the button say the thing: "Book a 20-minute property walk." Never "Submit."

Then decide where the page lives. On a site you own, not a social profile you rent. If you do not have a page you control yet, the free build at /free-build exists for exactly this: one page, on your domain, that you own.

What good looks like: the same button at the top and the bottom, a booking link or a short form behind it, and a plain sentence next to it about what happens next. "You will get a text to confirm the time, and I will bring last month's sample report."

The mistake: the second option. Every extra choice on the page is a place to stop. If you must offer a phone number for people who hate forms, fine, but it leads to the same next step, a walk-through, just booked by phone.

Example: Whitfield Family Dental, Marshall, fictional. The old page had "Call now," "Request appointment," "Ask a question," and a chat bubble. The new page has one: "Book a new patient visit." The front desk gets the same conversation every time.

Now run the prompt with your approved brief pasted in. It returns a page with every section. Then do the part most people skip. Read it aloud, start to finish, into your phone's voice recorder. Every place you stumble is a sentence to cut. Every place you sound like a brochure is a sentence to rewrite in your own words. Then revise once.

## Worked example

Pine Curve Lawn and Landscape, Gilmer. Fictional. Dale put the buyer brief, result statement, scope card, proof inventory, price worksheet, and objection bank into one Google Doc and read it through. He found one contradiction: the result statement from lesson two still said next-business-day response, and the scope card from lesson three said same-day during business hours. He kept the scope card's version and fixed the result statement. Then he pasted the whole brief into the exact working prompt.

Example output:

```text
Headline: Common areas that get done on schedule, with photos to prove it.
Problem: Your mow crew skipped a week again. Residents complained at the meeting. You have a day job, and you are chasing a vendor on your lunch break.
Outcome: Within 30 days you will have a written 12-month schedule, your first two visits completed with dated photos, and one number that answers, so you stop being the one who chases.
Deliverables: 12-month schedule (PDF). Weekly visit with signed checklist. Photo log after every visit. Monthly walk-through with a one-page report. One contact line, same-day response in business hours.
Proof: Trusted by boards across Upshur County. Fully insured. Serving Gilmer since 2019.
Price: $1,250 per month. Founding price $1,050 per month for the first three properties, with permission to photograph and one written reference.
Call to action: Book a 20-minute property walk. Or call for a quote.
```

Review against the pass standard. Does the page match the approved brief? Every line traced back to an artifact except one. Does it use one call to action? No. The walk button at the top and "or call for a quote" at the bottom is two. Does it make any unsupported claim? "Trusted by boards across Upshur County" is not in the proof inventory. It sounds good, and it is not a fact.

The one revision: Dale cut "or call for a quote," repeated the walk button at the bottom, and replaced the Upshur County line with "14 properties under contract, no missed scheduled visits since January 2025." Then he read the whole page aloud into his phone. Four minutes. He cut nine sentences that sounded like a website and kept the ones that sounded like him talking to a board president. The page got shorter and better.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Write a one-page offer page using this approved brief: [paste brief]. Include headline, problem, outcome, deliverables, process, proof, fit and non-fit, price, FAQ, limitations, and one call to action. Use short sentences and no hype.
```

## Guided practice

1. Paste the six artifacts from lessons one through six into one document and fix any line that contradicts another.
2. Paste the brief into the exact working prompt in ChatGPT or Claude and put the result into a fresh document.
3. Check every claim on the page against the proof inventory and delete anything with no source.
4. Cut to one call to action, name it as a verb, and repeat it at the top and the bottom.
5. Read the page aloud once into your phone, then cut every sentence you stumbled on.

## Pass standard

The page matches the approved brief, uses one call to action, and makes no unsupported claim.

## Common mistakes

- A headline about you instead of the buyer's problem. Fix: write the first line in words from your objection bank.
- Price hidden behind "contact us." Fix: put the number on the page with the founding condition next to it.
- Three ways to get in touch. Fix: one next step, one button, repeated twice.
- New claims that appear during drafting. Fix: every sentence in the proof section needs a line in the inventory.
- Publishing on a page you rent. Fix: put it on a domain you own; use /free-build if you have nothing yet.
