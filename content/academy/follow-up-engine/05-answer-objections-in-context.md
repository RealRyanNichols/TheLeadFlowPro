# FU L05: Answer Objections in Context

## What you will finish

Turn price, timing, trust, and fit questions into clear next decisions. You will have ten approved objection cards, each with the concern in the buyer's words, a direct answer, proof or source, a limitation, and a low-pressure next question, that anyone on your team can send.

## Why this matters

An objection is a reply. A lead who says "that is more than I expected" is talking to you. That is further than most of your leads ever get. What happens next decides whether you get the work.

Most businesses handle objections one of two ways, and both lose. The first is to argue. "Well, our quality is higher." The lead did not ask about quality. Now they feel dumb and defensive. The second is to fold. Drop the price, cave on timing, promise something you cannot deliver. You win the job and lose the margin, or you win the job and lose the customer when the promise breaks.

Picture a fence company in Hallsville. Their quote is $1,200 higher than the competitor's. The office manager, new, replies "we use better materials." No proof, no detail, no question. The lead goes with the cheaper guy. The right card would have said what the $1,200 buys, admitted what it does not, and asked one question.

You have the sequence from lesson three and the no-response messages from lesson four. This lesson is for when they do respond, with a concern. If you did the Offer Engine, bring your objection bank from OE L06. These cards are the follow-up version, written to be sent, not just understood.

## The lesson

### 1. Repeat the concern accurately

Before you answer anything, say back what they said, in their words. Not a softened version. Not your interpretation.

They wrote "you're $1,200 higher than the other quote." Your card opens with "You said we came in about $1,200 higher than the other quote." Not "I understand price is a concern." That sentence is what a script sounds like. Repeating their words proves you read it and lowers the temperature.

This is why the prompt asks for the concern in the buyer's words. Go collect them. Pull ten real objections from your inbox, your text threads in Quo, your call notes. Write them down as they were said, typos and all. If you do not have ten yet, ask your team what they hear most. Price, timing, trust, and fit cover most of it: "too expensive," "we want to wait until spring," "how do I know you'll show up," "we were thinking chain link, not wood."

What good looks like: the lead reads the first line of your reply and thinks "yes, that is what I said." The mistake: answering the objection you wish they had. They said price. You answered quality. Now you are talking past each other.

### 2. Answer with evidence and limits

Answer the actual question directly, then back it with something real, then say what the answer does not cover.

Direct answer first. "The $1,200 difference is mostly the posts. We set steel posts in concrete instead of wood. In this clay, wood posts tend to start leaning in a few years." Example. That is an answer. It says what the money buys.

Then evidence. Proof means something you can show. A photo of a fence you built five years ago. The materials line on the quote. A named supplier. A review with a date. A specific past job the lead could drive past. Never a number you cannot back up. The prompt says proof or source because the lead has to be able to check it, or at least see it.

Then the limitation. This is the part most people skip, and it is the part that builds trust. "If you plan to sell the house in two years, the cheaper fence probably makes more sense." Saying that costs you some jobs. It wins you the ones where you are the right fit, and those customers refer people.

Here is the rule I hold to on every card: no card invents proof. If you do not have the photo, do not describe the photo. If you do not know how long wood posts last, do not state a number. Say "in my experience" and mean it. Making up evidence in a follow-up is how a small business ends up with a one-star review that starts with "they told me."

What good looks like: the answer would hold up if the lead forwarded it to a contractor friend. The mistake: burying the answer in a paragraph of "we pride ourselves on."

### 3. Offer the next decision

End every card with a question that moves the lead toward a decision. Small, and without pressure.

The next decision is not always "buy." After a price objection, the next decision might be "which of these two versions fits your budget?" After timing: "want me to check back in March, or hold a spring date now at this price?" After trust: "would it help to talk to the customer on Pine Tree Road who had the same worry?" After fit: "want a quote on the chain link version so you can compare?"

Notice that each one gives the lead a real choice, including a way out. Low pressure means they can pick "neither" and you would still be fine.

Now build the cards as a set. Ten cards. Each one fits on a phone screen. Each one is approved by the owner before anyone sends it. Store them where the team already works: a Notion page, a ClickUp doc, or a snippet folder in Quo. When a lead objects, the team member finds the card, personalizes the first line with what the lead actually said, and sends it by the channel the lead used. Then logs it, which is lesson six.

What good looks like: a new hire could send the card on day one and not embarrass you. The mistake: cards that argue. Any card containing "actually," "with respect," or "as I mentioned" needs a rewrite. You are not winning a debate. You are helping someone decide.

## Worked example

Sabine Fence and Deck in Hallsville is a fictional example. Owner plus two crews, and an office manager who handles email and Quo. Objections collected from the last month of replies, pasted into the prompt:

"Create response cards for these objections: 'you're $1,200 higher than the other quote,' 'we want to wait until spring,' 'how do I know you'll actually show up,' 'we were thinking chain link, not wood.' Each card needs the concern in the buyer's words, a direct answer, proof or source, limitation, and a low-pressure next question."

Example output (price card):

```text
Concern: "You're $1,200 higher than the other quote."
Answer: Most of that is the posts. We set steel posts in concrete; the other quote lists treated wood posts.
Proof: Line 3 of our quote shows the post spec. Photo of the Kimbrough fence, built 2019, still straight, in the gallery link.
Limitation: If you plan to move within a few years, the wood-post fence may be the better spend.
Next question: Want me to price a wood-post version so you can compare both side by side?
```

Review against the pass standard. Invents proof: yes. The "Kimbrough fence, built 2019" does not exist. The model made up a customer. That fails. Argues with the lead: no. Hides a limitation: no, the move-soon limit is there and it is honest.

The one revision: the owner replaced the invented job with a real one, a fence off Loop 281 he built in 2021, and added the actual photo link from his gallery. He also changed "the other quote lists treated wood posts" to "check whether the other quote specifies the post material," because he had not seen the other quote and could not say what was in it. Ten cards, all approved, saved in Notion where the office manager already works.

## Guided practice

1. Collect ten real objections from your inbox, Quo messages, and call notes, in the buyer's exact words.
2. Sort them into price, timing, trust, and fit, and note any that do not fit.
3. Run the exact prompt with all ten pasted in.
4. Check every proof line: if you cannot show it, replace it with something you can or delete it.
5. Read each card aloud as if you were the lead, and rewrite any that sound like an argument.

## Assignment and evidence

Build ten approved objection cards for your team.

Save the source material, the prompt, the first result, the final result, and your review notes.

## Pass standard

No card invents proof, argues with the lead, or hides a meaningful limitation.

## Common mistakes

- Answering the objection you wish they had. Fix: open the card with their exact words.
- Proof that does not exist. Fix: real photo, real line item, real named job, or nothing.
- No limitation. Fix: every card says who this answer is not for.
- Cards that argue. Fix: delete "actually," "with respect," and "as I mentioned," then reread.
- Cards the owner never approved. Fix: approve all ten before the team sends one.
