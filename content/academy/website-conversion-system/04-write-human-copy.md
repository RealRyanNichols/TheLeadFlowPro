# WC L04: Write Human Copy

## What you will finish

Turn the approved brief into specific, plain-language website copy. When you are done you will have the full page written, read aloud, and revised, with every claim tied to proof and every missing proof flagged instead of faked.

## Why this matters

Most website copy sounds like it was written by a company, for a company. "We are a full-service roofing contractor committed to excellence and customer satisfaction." Nobody talks like that. Nobody reads it either. The visitor's eyes slide right over it looking for something real.

Here is the scene. A roofer in Longview has a page like that. Then he tells me how he actually explains it on a porch: "I will get on the roof, take photos of what I find, and show you on my phone before I tell you what it costs. If it is fine, I will tell you it is fine." That is copy. That belongs on the page. It came out of his mouth, not a template.

Copy is the part of the site the visitor actually reads. The layout gets them to look. The words get them to act. Vague copy makes a good offer look like every other offer. Specific copy makes an ordinary offer look like the obvious choice.

You built the wireframe in lesson three so the copy has a place to live. Now you fill it in, section by section, with words a real person would say to another real person.

## The lesson

### 1. Use the buyer's language

Bring the annotated wireframe from lesson three and the proof inventory from OE L04 if you have one. Also bring anything the buyer has ever said to you in their own words: texts, reviews, emails, the questions they ask on the phone. That is your source material, and it is worth more than any writing trick.

The visitor does not say "residential roofing solutions." They say "my roof is leaking and I do not know if insurance covers it." Write the page in the second sentence, not the first.

Start by collecting the buyer's words. Open your reviews and copy out the phrases people use. Read your last twenty texts from customers. If you did OA03, the buyer page from OE L01 already has this. Write the phrases in a list. "Showed up when they said." "Did not try to upsell me." "Explained it so I could understand." Those go into your copy, in that form.

Then write each section of the wireframe as if you were explaining it to that person on their porch. Short sentences. "You" and "we." Say what happens. No adjective you could not defend to a skeptic.

What good looks like: a customer reads it and says "that sounds like you." The mistake is writing to impress other roofers. Your competitors are not your buyers. Write to the homeowner.

### 2. Prefer specifics

Every vague claim on a page is an invitation to stop trusting you. "Fast service" means nothing. "We answer the phone during business hours and get to Gregg County roofs within two business days" means something, if it is true.

Go through the draft and hunt for vague words. Quality, professional, reliable, affordable, trusted, experienced, best. Each time you find one, ask: what is the specific fact behind this? Replace the word with the fact. If there is no fact, cut the word.

Specifics include numbers you can back up, names of places, what is included, what is not included, what it costs or the range, and how long things take. "Same-week install on most water heaters, two-year labor warranty in writing" beats "quality plumbing you can trust" every time.

Proof-linked claims mean every claim points at something. "Rated 4.9 on Google from 84 reviews" points at the Google profile. "Licensed, TX license number here" points at the state lookup. If you cannot point at something, you are asking the visitor to take your word, and they have no reason to.

This is where the prompt earns its keep. Tell it to flag missing proof instead of inventing it. ChatGPT and Claude will happily write "hundreds of satisfied customers" if you let them. Do not let them. The output should say "[proof needed: review count]" and you go get the count.

The mistake: rounding up. If you have 84 reviews, say 84, not "nearly 100." Small honesty builds the trust the whole page runs on.

Example: "Serving East Texas for years" becomes "Roofing in Gregg and Harrison County since 2014," because the owner has the 2014 license date to point at.

### 3. Remove unsupported superlatives

Best. Number one. Top-rated. Leading. Premier. Unless you can show who ranked you and how, these words do the opposite of what you want. Visitors have seen them on every site. They read as noise, or worse, as a sign you have nothing real to say.

Read the draft aloud. Actually aloud, with your mouth. Every place you would feel a little silly saying the sentence to a customer's face, rewrite it. Every sentence longer than a breath, split it. Every heading that could sit on a competitor's site, make it specific to yours.

Then check the page against the pass standard. Hand it to someone who does not know your business. Give them sixty seconds on a phone. Take it away and ask four questions: what is the offer, who is it for, why should I believe it, and what do I do next. If they miss one, fix that section, not the whole page.

The one primary call to action from the wireframe stays one. Do not let the copy sneak in a "or call us, or email us, or message us on Facebook" paragraph. One main action, one softer secondary action, the same words at each decision point.

What good looks like: a page with no word you would not say out loud, no claim you cannot point at, and one clear next step. The mistake is polishing forever. Two passes: one for specifics, one for reading aloud. Then it goes to build.

One more scene to keep in mind. A fence company in Henderson had "Quality craftsmanship at competitive prices" on its page. We replaced it with "Cedar privacy fences, installed in three days or less, priced per foot before we start." Same business, same prices. The second one gets the call.

## Worked example

Ridge Line Roofing in Longview is a fictional example. An owner and a crew of four. Offer: a free roof inspection with photos, aimed at homeowners after storm season. Wireframe from lesson three in hand. Source material: twelve Google reviews copied into a doc, the license number, the real review count (84, with a 4.9 average), and a note that the owner does not yet have a count of inspections completed this year.

He ran the lesson prompt with the wireframe and the reviews pasted in:

"Write the full page from this wireframe and source material: [wireframe and twelve reviews]. Use short sentences, concrete nouns, proof-linked claims, useful headings, one primary call to action, and no hype. Flag any missing proof instead of inventing it."

Example output:

```text
Headline: Get photos of your roof before anyone tells you what it costs.
Support: Free inspection for homeowners in Gregg and Harrison County. We climb up, photograph what we find, and show you on the spot.
Proof: 84 Google reviews, 4.9 average. Licensed, TX license [number]. [Proof needed: inspections completed this year.]
Process: 1. Book a time. 2. We inspect and photograph. 3. You see the photos before any estimate. 4. We provide an honest assessment.
Fit: Homeowners with a roof over ten years old or any storm in the last year. Not for new construction.
CTA: Book a free inspection.
```

Review against the pass standard. Could a visitor explain the offer, fit, evidence, and next step after a sixty-second scan? We tested it with the owner's neighbor on her phone. Offer, yes. Fit, yes, she said "Gregg and Harrison County, older roofs." Evidence, yes, she remembered the review count. Next step, yes. It passes. The proof flag stayed in as a flag, and the owner is pulling the inspection count from his invoices.

The one revision: process step four, "We provide an honest assessment," is the kind of line that sits on every roofing site. The owner's real porch line was "if it is fine, I will tell you it is fine." We used his words. When the neighbor handed the phone back, that was the line she quoted.

```text
Write the full page from this wireframe and source material: [paste]. Use short sentences, concrete nouns, proof-linked claims, useful headings, one primary call to action, and no hype. Flag any missing proof instead of inventing it.
```

## Guided practice

1. Copy ten phrases from real reviews and customer texts into a list of buyer language.
2. Run the prompt with the wireframe and the buyer language pasted in as source material.
3. Hunt the draft for vague words and replace each one with a fact or cut it.
4. Read the whole page aloud and rewrite anything you would not say to a customer's face.
5. Give the page to someone outside the business for sixty seconds and ask the four questions.

## Assignment and evidence

Draft, read aloud, and revise the page copy.

Save the source material, the prompt, the first result, the final result, and your review notes.

## Pass standard

A visitor can explain the offer, fit, evidence, and next step after a sixty-second scan.

## Common mistakes

- Company voice. Fix: write it the way you say it on the porch, with "you" and "we."
- Vague words doing the work. Fix: replace quality, reliable, and professional with the fact behind them.
- Letting the AI invent proof. Fix: require "proof needed" flags and go get the real number.
- Rounding up. Fix: 84 reviews is 84 reviews.
- Three calls to action in the closing paragraph. Fix: one main action, one secondary, same words throughout.
