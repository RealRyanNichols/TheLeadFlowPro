# WC L02: Map the Site Path

## What you will finish

Build the shortest clear route from arrival to result. When you are done you will have two drawings, the path visitors take today and the path you are going to build, with every dead end marked and every entry point landing somewhere useful.

## Why this matters

A visitor does not see your site the way you do. You see a menu with eight items. They see one link they clicked and a question they need answered. If the page they land on does not move them toward an answer, they hit the back button, and you never know they were there.

Here is a scene from a lawn care company in Hallsville. Their Google Business Profile points to the homepage. The homepage has a Services button. Services lists six things with no prices and no button. The visitor finds Contact in the menu, fills out the form, and gets a page that says "Thanks!" and nothing else. Nobody replies for four days because the form emails an inbox nobody checks. That is four dead ends in one trip. The visitor did everything right and still got nothing.

The site path is the map of every route a visitor can take from the moment they arrive to the moment they get a result or leave on purpose. Lesson one gave each page a job. This lesson connects the jobs so the visitor never has to guess the next step.

You need this before the wireframe in lesson three, because the layout of a page depends on what the visitor saw right before it and where they go after.

## The lesson

### 1. List entry points

Bring the page-job briefs from lesson one. Each one has an arrival source and a success event. The site path is everything that happens between those two things, for every way in.

An entry point is any place a visitor can start. Your Google Business Profile link. A Meta ad. The link in your email signature. A QR code on the truck. The text you send after a missed call. A Google search for "fence company Henderson." Each one drops the visitor onto a specific page with a specific question.

Write every entry point down, and next to each one write the page it lands on today. Be honest. If your ad sends people to the homepage, write homepage, even if you know it should be the offer page.

Then trace one visitor from each entry point by hand. Open a private browser window, click the actual link, and go where a visitor would go. Write each page and each click. This takes ten minutes and finds more problems than any tool I have used.

What good looks like: a list where every entry point lands on the page whose job matches the visitor's question. Ad for the offer lands on the offer page. Missed-call text lands on the booking page. Google Business Profile lands on the homepage, and the first screen of the homepage points at the main offer.

The mistake: sending every source to the homepage because that is the only link you remember. Every entry point should land on the page built for that visitor.

### 2. Remove dead ends

A dead end is any page or moment where the visitor has no obvious next step. A services page with no button. A blog post with no offer. A thank-you page that only says thanks. A form error that clears everything they typed. A menu with eight equal items and no hint about which one matters.

Circular navigation is a dead end too. Homepage sends to Services, Services sends to Contact, Contact sends back to the homepage, and the visitor has gone in a loop without reaching a result.

Go through your traced paths and mark every dead end. Then for each one, decide: does this page need a next step, or does it need to go away? Most small business sites have pages that exist because a template came with them. A page nobody visits, that answers no question, can be deleted. Fewer pages, fewer places to get lost.

Now look for unnecessary steps. If a visitor has to click Services, then Fencing, then Get a Quote, then fill out a form, that is three clicks before the job even starts. Could the offer page hold the form? Usually yes.

What good looks like: every page ends with one clear next action, and the main route from your biggest entry point to the success event is two clicks or fewer. The mistake is fixing a dead end by adding more links. Add one clear next step, not five options.

Example: a services page with six blurbs and no button. Fix: one line under each blurb, "Get a quote," pointing at the same quote page. Or fold the six blurbs into the quote page and delete Services.

### 3. Connect confirmation and follow-up

The path does not end when the form submits. It ends when the visitor gets a result. That means the thank-you page and the first follow-up message are part of the site path, and you draw them on the map.

The thank-you page confirms what just happened, says exactly what happens next and when, and gives one next action for the eager ones, like a booking link or the phone number. It also fires the success event from lesson one.

Then the follow-up. If you did OA05 The Follow-Up Engine, you already wrote the first response in FU L02. Connect it here. Form submitted, record created, first text or email goes out inside the window you promised on the thank-you page. If you promised one business hour, a person or a system has to hit that.

If you have not built follow-up yet, write what happens today. "Form emails Ryan, Ryan replies when he sees it." That is the truth, and it belongs on the current path drawing so you can see the gap.

Draw two versions. The current path, with the dead ends and loops marked. The proposed path, with each entry point landing on the right page, each page pointing to one next step, and confirmation plus follow-up connected at the end. Boxes and arrows on paper are fine. A photo of the whiteboard is fine. The workbook has a template.

What good looks like: you can put your finger on any entry point and trace it to a result without lifting it. The mistake is drawing the proposed path and skipping the current one. You need both, because the current one shows you what to cut.

If you are not sure which part of the business is leaking, the Business Growth Diagnostic at /diagnostic is free and points at it.

## Worked example

Hallsville Lawn and Land is a fictional example. Two crews, weekly mowing and one-time cleanups. Entry points: Google Business Profile, a Facebook page, a yard sign with a QR code, and word of mouth. Current pages: home, services, gallery, about, contact.

The owner ran the lesson prompt with the buyer and offer filled in:

"Map a website journey for homeowners in Hallsville and Longview buying weekly mowing from Hallsville Lawn and Land. Start with entry sources and show pages, questions answered, decisions, form or checkout, thank-you page, and follow-up. Identify dead ends and unnecessary steps."

Example output:

```text
Entry: Google Business Profile to homepage. Question: do you serve my street and what does mowing cost? Homepage answers neither. Dead end.
Entry: yard sign QR to homepage. Same gap. Proposed: QR lands on a mowing quote page with a price range and the form.
Entry: Facebook to services page. Six items, no next step. Dead end.
Form to "Thanks!" page. No timing promise, no next action. Dead end.
Unnecessary: Gallery in the main menu with no page job. Fold three photos into the quote page, remove Gallery.
Proposed route: any entry to quote page, form, thank-you with "we text within one business hour," first text from the sequence.
```

Review against the pass standard. Does every entry point reach a useful result or a clear exit without circular navigation? On the proposed path, yes. Four entry points, one quote page, one form, one thank-you, one follow-up. The current path failed at three points, which is what we expected to find.

The one revision: the first proposed path sent the Google Business Profile visitor straight to the quote page. But a lot of those visitors are calling, not reading. We kept the homepage for that entry, put the phone number and a "Get a mowing quote" button in the first screen, and made the quote page one click away. Same result, without forcing a caller to read a form.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Map a website journey for [buyer and offer]. Start with entry sources and show pages, questions answered, decisions, form or checkout, thank-you page, and follow-up. Identify dead ends and unnecessary steps.
```

## Guided practice

1. List every entry point to your site and the page each one lands on today.
2. Open a private browser window and trace one visitor from each entry point, writing every click.
3. Run the prompt with your buyer and offer, and compare its dead ends to the ones you found.
4. Draw the current path with dead ends marked, then the proposed path with one next step per page.
5. Add the thank-you page and the first follow-up message to the end of the proposed path.

## Assignment and evidence

Draw the current and proposed site paths.

Save the source material, the prompt, the first result, the final result, and your review notes.

## Pass standard

Every entry point reaches a useful result or clear exit without circular navigation.

## Common mistakes

- Sending every entry point to the homepage. Fix: land each source on the page built for that visitor's question.
- Fixing a dead end by adding more links. Fix: one clear next step per page.
- Ending the map at the form. Fix: draw the thank-you page and the first follow-up as part of the path.
- Drawing only the proposed path. Fix: draw the current path first so you can see what to cut.
- Keeping template pages nobody visits. Fix: delete pages that have no job and no traffic.
