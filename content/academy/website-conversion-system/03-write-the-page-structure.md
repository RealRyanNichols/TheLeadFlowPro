# WC L03: Write the Page Structure

## What you will finish

Arrange problem, result, proof, process, fit, FAQ, and action in a scannable order. When you are done you will have an annotated wireframe for your offer page: every section in order, each with its purpose, headline, supporting copy, proof, visual, and call to action written in, before a single line of final copy exists.

## Why this matters

Most offer pages are written in the order the owner thinks about the business. Who we are, what we do, how long we have been doing it, and somewhere near the bottom, a button. The visitor reads in a different order. They want to know if you understand their problem, what they get, whether to believe you, what happens next, and whether this is for someone like them. Then they act.

Picture a gym in Tyler. The owner writes a page that opens with the story of how she started the gym. Real, heartfelt, and nobody reads it, because the visitor came in asking "will I be embarrassed walking in there, and how much is it?" The page answers that in paragraph six. Most visitors never get there on a phone.

A wireframe is the skeleton of the page. Sections in order, each with a purpose, before the copy exists. It costs an hour and it saves you from writing good copy in the wrong place.

Lesson one gave the page a job. Lesson two put the page in the path. This lesson decides what goes on the page and in what order, so that in lesson four the copy has a place to live.

## The lesson

### 1. Lead with relevance

You need three inputs. The page-job brief from lesson one. The offer brief from OA03 The Offer Engine, which is the offer page wireframe from OE L07 if you have it, or your buyer, result, scope, proof, price, and next action written on one page if you do not. And the proof inventory from OE L04 if you built one. The wireframe cannot be better than the offer it holds, so get those in front of you first.

The standard order for a conversion page: problem, result, proof, process, fit, FAQ, action. You will bend it for your page, but start there.

The first screen on a phone is all most visitors see before deciding whether to keep going. It has to prove, in about five seconds, that the visitor is in the right place. That means the headline names their problem or their result in their words, and the line under it says who this is for.

Not "Welcome to Ironwood Strength." Say "Six weeks of coached training for adults who have not been in a gym in years." Now the visitor who fits leans in, and the visitor who does not leaves without wasting anyone's time.

Under the headline: one supporting line, one proof element (a real photo or a real number you can back up), and the main action button. That is the whole first screen. Put the button where a thumb can reach it.

What good looks like: someone reads only the first screen and can say what this is and who it is for. The mistake is opening with your name, your logo, and a slogan. Your name is in the header. The first screen is about the visitor.

### 2. Answer risk before asking

After relevance comes the visitor's biggest worry. Every offer has one. For the gym it is "will I be embarrassed." For a roofer it is "will you show up, and will insurance cover this." For a lawyer it is "what will this cost me, and will you take my call."

The sections between the first screen and the next call to action exist to answer that worry with proof, then show the process, then confirm fit.

Proof means things you can back up. Real reviews with names and towns. Photos of your actual work. A license number. A count you can verify, like "212 roofs in Gregg County since 2019," if that number is true and you can show the records. If you do not have proof for a claim, the wireframe says "proof needed" in that slot, and you either go find it or cut the claim. Do not let the wireframe pretend.

Process means "here is what happens after you tap the button." Three or four steps, one sentence each. This section removes more hesitation than any testimonial, because it tells the visitor what they are agreeing to.

Fit means who this is for and who it is not for. Saying who it is not for builds trust faster than anything else on the page, and it keeps the wrong leads out of your inbox.

The mistake is putting the ask before the answer. A button in the first screen is fine for the visitor who arrived ready. A second button right after the first paragraph, before any proof, just gets ignored.

Example: a roofer's worry section. Headline: "You see the photos before you see a price." Proof: two reviews that mention the photos. Then the process: book, we inspect, you see photos, you decide.

### 3. Repeat the action at decision points

The call to action does not go after every section. It goes where a visitor would naturally decide. The first screen, for the ones who arrived ready. After proof and process, for the ones who needed convincing. After the FAQ, for the ones who needed one last question answered. Three spots on a typical page. Same button, same words, every time.

If the button says "Book a free intro session" at the top, it says "Book a free intro session" at the bottom. Different wording in different places makes the visitor wonder if they are different things.

The FAQ is where objections go. Take the objection bank from OE L06 if you have it. Five to seven questions in the visitor's words, one short answer each. Price, timing, what if it does not work for me, what do I need to bring, can I cancel.

Now build the wireframe. One block per section. For each: purpose, headline, supporting copy notes, proof, visual, and call to action. The prompt below produces this from your offer brief. Your job is to check the order against the visitor's questions and cut anything that does not serve the page job.

Keep mobile scanning in mind the whole way. Every section needs a heading that means something on its own, because most people read headings and skip the rest. If someone reads only the headings, they should still get the pitch.

What good looks like: a page that reads in the order the visitor asks questions, with a button only where they would decide. The mistake is a wireframe with twelve sections. Seven is usually plenty. If you cannot say what question a section answers, delete it.

## Worked example

Ironwood Strength in Tyler is a fictional example. A small coached-training gym. Offer: a six-week starter program for adults who have not trained in years. Offer brief from OA03 in hand. The page-job brief says the visitor arrives from a Meta ad and the success event is a booked intro session. The owner has three real reviews and member photos, but no completion numbers she can prove yet.

She ran the lesson prompt:

"Create a conversion-focused wireframe for the six-week starter program page for Ironwood Strength in Tyler using the approved offer brief. For each section give purpose, headline, supporting copy, proof, visual, and call to action. Keep mobile scanning in mind."

Example output:

```text
1. First screen. Purpose: relevance. Headline: Six weeks of coached training for adults who have not been in a gym in years. Proof: photo of a regular member mid-session. CTA: Book a free intro session.
2. The worry. Purpose: answer the fear of being judged. Copy: small groups, a coach beside you every session. Proof: two reviews that mention feeling welcome. CTA: Book a free intro session.
3. What you get. Purpose: result and scope. Twelve coached sessions, a written plan, weekly check-ins. Proof needed: completion count.
4. How it works. Purpose: process. Book the intro, meet the coach, first session within a week. CTA: Book a free intro session.
5. Who it is for and not for. Purpose: fit. No CTA.
6. FAQ. Price, schedule, old injuries, what to wear, can I pause.
7. Final action. Same CTA.
```

Review against the pass standard. Does the order follow the visitor's questions? Yes. The worry comes right after relevance, process comes before fit, and the FAQ sits just before the final ask. Does the call to action appear only at logical decision points? No. Section two has a button right after the fear section, before the visitor knows what they get or how it works.

The one revision: remove the CTA from section two. The button now appears in the first screen, after "how it works," and after the FAQ. A visitor sees it when they know what they are agreeing to. That is the decision point, not the fear point. The section three slot still says "proof needed," and the owner is counting completions from her sign-in sheets before the copy gets written.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Create a conversion-focused wireframe for [offer page] using the approved offer brief. For each section give purpose, headline, supporting copy, proof, visual, and call to action. Keep mobile scanning in mind.
```

## Guided practice

1. Put the page-job brief and the offer brief side by side and write the visitor's biggest worry in one sentence.
2. Run the prompt with your offer page named and the offer brief pasted in.
3. Reorder the sections so relevance comes first and the worry is answered before the next ask.
4. Mark every call to action and delete any that is not at a decision point.
5. Write "proof needed" in any slot where you do not yet have real evidence.

## Pass standard

The order follows the visitor's questions and the call to action appears only at logical decision points.

## Common mistakes

- Opening with your name and a slogan. Fix: the first screen names the visitor's problem or result and who it is for.
- A button after every section. Fix: first screen, after process, after FAQ, same words each time.
- Claims with no proof slot. Fix: write "proof needed" and go find it or cut the claim.
- Headings that say nothing, like "Our Approach." Fix: headings that carry the pitch on their own.
- Twelve sections. Fix: if you cannot name the question a section answers, delete it.
