# LC L02: Design the Lead Magnet

## What you will finish

Create the checklist, calculator, prompt pack, diagnostic, or mini-course outline. When you are done you will have version one of the actual lead magnet, built and tested by you on your own phone, ready to hand to a stranger.

## Why this matters

The promise from lesson one is a sentence. This lesson turns it into a thing. This is where most people stall, because building feels bigger than choosing.

Here is what stalling costs. A detailer in Hallsville had "free ceramic coating guide, coming soon" on his site for seven months. Seven months of ad clicks and drive-by traffic hitting a promise with nothing behind it. The form was live. The download was not. He collected forty emails and sent them nothing.

The other failure is the opposite. The owner builds a beautiful twenty-page PDF with a table of contents. Nobody finishes page three. A lead magnet the buyer does not finish never reaches the last step, and the last step is where the bridge to your offer lives.

We build this before the form because the form asks for something in exchange. You need to know exactly what you are handing over, in what format, before you decide what to ask for.

## The lesson

### 1. Choose the right format

Five formats cover almost every small business. Checklist: a list the buyer works through and ticks off. Calculator: the buyer enters two or three numbers and gets one number back. Prompt pack: five to ten prompts the buyer pastes into ChatGPT to do a job. Diagnostic: ten to twelve questions with a score and a reading at the end. Mini-course outline: three to five short steps with one action each.

Match the format to the micro result, not to what looks impressive. If the result is a decision, use a diagnostic. If the result is a number, use a calculator. If the result is a done task, use a checklist. If the result is a written thing, use a prompt pack. If the result is a habit over a few days, use a mini-course outline.

What good looks like: the format is obvious once you say the result out loud. "Find the age and risk score of your water heater" is a checklist with a score line at the bottom. The mistake people make: choosing a mini-course because it feels more valuable. A mini-course is five chances for the buyer to quit.

Example: our free calculators at /tools are lead magnets in the calculator format. The buyer types in numbers, gets a number back, and the number tells them whether they have a problem. If you want to build a calculator without code, look at the Tool Studio at /go/tools. For everything else, a one-page Google Doc exported to PDF is fine for version one.

### 2. Remove unnecessary content

Run the prompt with your format, buyer, and micro result. You get a full outline: title, instructions, steps, examples, completion check, and next step. It will be too long. They always are.

Now cut. Go line by line and ask one question of each line: does the buyer need this to finish? Company history, no. Why this matters, no, they already know or they would not have downloaded it. Three ways to do step two, no, pick one. Definitions of words the buyer already uses, no.

Keep the instructions, the steps, one example per step where the step is not obvious, and the completion check. The completion check is the line that says "you are done when you have X." Do not cut that. It is how the buyer knows they finished.

What good looks like: the whole thing fits on one page, or one screen on a phone, with room to write. Twenty minutes to finish, start to end. The mistake people make: leaving in the part they are proud of. If it does not help the buyer finish, it goes.

Example: a lawn company in Gilmer had a spring lawn checklist with an intro paragraph about soil science. Cut the paragraph. The checklist went from two pages to one, and the completion check moved to the top of the page where the buyer sees it first.

### 3. End with a useful next decision

The last step of the lead magnet is a decision, not a pitch. The buyer finished. They have a score, a number, a list. Now tell them what that result means and what a reasonable person does next in each case.

Write it as two or three branches. If your score is under 4, you are fine, recheck in a year. If it is 4 to 7, do these two things yourself this month. If it is 8 or more, this is the point where a professional look makes sense, and here is how to get one.

Notice the paid offer is only in one branch. That is what non-pushy means. The buyer who is fine gets told they are fine. That buyer tells their neighbor about you. The buyer whose score is high gets a real next step, the one you built the offer for in the Offer Engine.

What good looks like: every branch has an action. The mistake people make: ending with "contact us for a free consultation" for everyone, which turns a useful tool back into a flyer.

Example: the detailer's diagnostic ends with "Under 5: a wash and wax will hold you. 5 to 8: book a paint correction. 9 or more: correction plus coating, and here is a ten-minute call to confirm." One branch points at the paid offer. The others still help.

Then build version one. Google Doc, Canva, Notion page, or a page on your site. Do every instruction yourself, on your phone, with a timer running. If you cannot finish in twenty minutes with only what is on the page, a stranger cannot either. Fix it and run it again.

## Worked example

Piney Woods Detail Co. in Hallsville is a fictional example. One bay, one owner, trucks all day. They sell paint correction and ceramic coating. Lesson one produced this promise: "Find out in ten minutes whether your truck needs a coating, a correction, or just a good wash."

The owner chose the diagnostic format because the result is a decision. He ran the lesson prompt with the brackets filled in:

"Build a complete outline for a twelve-question diagnostic that helps a truck owner in East Texas who drives it daily produce a clear answer on wash, correction, or coating. Include title, instructions, steps, examples, completion check, and a non-pushy next step. Keep it usable in twenty minutes."

Example output:

```text
Title: The Ten-Minute Truck Paint Check
Instructions: Park in shade. Answer each question yes or no. Count your yes answers.
Q1. Run your fingernail across a scratch. Does it catch?
Q2. Do you see swirl marks in direct sun?
Q3. Has the truck been through an automatic brush wash more than ten times?
Completion check: You are done when you have a number from 0 to 12.
Next step: 0 to 4, wash and wax. 5 to 8, paint correction. 9 to 12, correction and coating, book a ten-minute call.
```

Review against the pass standard. Can a new user complete it without a call? Yes, every question is a look or a touch. Without missing information? Almost. Question one assumed people know what "catch" means. Without a hidden paid requirement? Yes, the wash-and-wax branch sends them away with an answer and nothing to buy.

The one revision that made it better: the owner added one line under question one, "If your nail stops in the scratch, that is a yes," then tested the whole sheet on his brother-in-law's truck with a timer running. Eight minutes. Version one was done.

## Exact working prompt

```text
Build a complete outline for a [format] that helps [buyer] produce [micro-result]. Include title, instructions, steps, examples, completion check, and a non-pushy next step. Keep it usable in twenty minutes.
```

## Guided practice

1. Say your micro result out loud and pick the format that matches it: checklist, calculator, prompt pack, diagnostic, or mini-course outline.
2. Run the exact prompt with [format], [buyer], and [micro-result] filled in.
3. Cut every line the buyer does not need to finish, and keep the completion check.
4. Write the ending as two or three branches, with the paid offer in only one of them.
5. Build version one in a Google Doc, Canva, or Notion, then do every step yourself on your phone with a timer.

## Pass standard

A new user can complete it without a call, missing information, or hidden paid requirement.

## Common mistakes

- Building a mini-course when the result is a decision. Fix: match the format to the result, not to what sounds valuable.
- Leaving in the intro you are proud of. Fix: cut anything the buyer does not need to finish.
- No completion check. Fix: add the line "you are done when you have X."
- Every branch ends in "contact us." Fix: give the low-score buyer a real answer and put the offer in one branch.
- Never testing it yourself. Fix: run it on your phone with a timer before anyone else sees it.
