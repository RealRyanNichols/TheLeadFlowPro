# What a business website in Longview should actually do (and what it should cost)

Most business websites in Longview were built to exist. They have a home page, a page that says what the business does, a phone number somewhere, and a contact form that goes to an inbox nobody checks after five. That is a brochure. It is not doing a job.

A website that does a job answers three questions before a customer has to call: what do you do, what does it cost or how do I get a price, and how do I reach you right now from my phone. Then it hands the inquiry to a person with enough context to reply.

Here is what that looks like, what it should cost in 2026, and how to check whether yours is doing it.

## The five pages that matter

You do not need twenty pages. You need five that each do one thing.

1. **Home.** Who you serve, what you do, and the one next step. If a customer only reads the first screen on a phone, they should still know whether to call.
2. **Services.** Each service named the way customers say it, with what is included and how pricing works. "Call for a quote" is fine; "we do not list prices" with no explanation is not.
3. **About.** A real person, a real location, and why you do the work. In a town the size of Longview, people buy from people they can picture.
4. **Contact.** Click to call, click to text, a short form, hours, and the service area. On a phone, the call button should be reachable with one thumb.
5. **One conversion page.** A quote request, a booking page, a free estimate form, or a single campaign page for the offer you are running right now.

Everything else, the gallery, the reviews, the FAQ, lives inside those five or waits until the five are working.

## What "working" means

A website is working when you can trace an inquiry from where it came from to what happened next. That takes four small things:

- **A form that creates a record**, not just an email. The record has the person's name, what they asked, where they came from, and who is going to reply.
- **Lead routing** to the inbox or CRM you actually watch, with an alert to the person who answers the phone.
- **First-party analytics** in an account you own, so you can see which page and which source produced the inquiry.
- **Basic search foundation**: page titles that say what and where, a description for each page, LocalBusiness structured data, a sitemap, and the site submitted to Google.

If a site cannot tell you which inquiries it produced last month, it is not working, however good it looks.

## What you should own

This is the part local businesses get burned on. Ask whoever builds your site four questions:

- Is the domain registered in my name, in an account I control?
- Can I export the site, or is it locked inside a builder I rent?
- Do the form submissions and the customer list live somewhere I can log into without you?
- Is the analytics account mine?

If the answer to any of those is no, you are renting your front door. The monthly fee is the smaller cost. The bigger one is that leaving means starting over.

## What it costs

Three honest ranges, as of the date on this article.

- **Template you build yourself.** Tens of dollars a month for the builder, plus your evenings. Cheap to start, expensive to leave.
- **Freelancer or agency on a rented platform.** Commonly a few thousand dollars, then a monthly platform fee that never ends.
- **An owned build.** A fixed build cost, then small infrastructure costs you pay directly to the hosting and domain vendors.

## A five-minute check for the site you have

Open your site on your phone, not your desk.

- Can you tell what the business does in the first screen?
- Is there a call or text button you can press with your thumb?
- Fill in the contact form. Time how long until a person replies.
- Search your business name plus "Longview." Does your site come up, and does the title say what you do?
- Ask whoever built it: who owns the domain and where do the form submissions go?
